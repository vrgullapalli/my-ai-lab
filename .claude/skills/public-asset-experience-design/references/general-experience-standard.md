# General public asset experience standard

Applies to every public asset, lightweight or full. The design answers
these questions, in the person's terms, before anything is built.
Motto: **simple on the surface, rigorous underneath.**

## Why would someone start?
The recognizable situation that makes this worth attention today — not
a category label, their actual moment.

## What is the shortest path to value?
The least effort before meaningful value arrives. Everything that
lengthens this path must justify itself.

## What is the first value moment?
Define explicitly: the first instant the person should think "this is
useful." Design backward from it. If it sits behind three screens of
setup, the design is wrong.

## What must the user provide?
Only what materially changes the result. Nothing collected because it
might be useful later. Every ask is a cost charged against trust.

## What happens during use?
The person makes useful progress WHILE the asset learns about them —
never data collection followed by a delayed payoff.

## What do they leave with?
A result that is clear, tangible, usable, and proportionate to what
they provided. A copyable, forwardable result beats a screen state.

## What can they do next?
The next action follows from what was learned. Options, not a
predetermined funnel — the settled ruling: the next capability may be
another asset, a conversation, a paid diagnostic, or nothing.

## What should be hidden?
System machinery, internal taxonomy, implementation vocabulary, audit
detail, and bulk evidence live underneath — reachable when the person
deliberately asks for depth, invisible otherwise.

## Flow representation
For meaningful experiences: why start → what do I provide → what
happens → first useful realization → what do I receive → what can I do
with it → what becomes relevant next. Use the smallest representation
that makes the experience clear; a journey map is not a deliverable by
default.

## Information hierarchy
Per screen or state, one question: **what is the one thing that should
win?** Fail states to design out: everything equal weight; competing
explanations; the system describing itself; the important thing
halfway down; the user inferring what matters. Three layers — primary
(what matters now), secondary (why), tertiary (evidence, method,
provenance) — and the tertiary layer is never required reading for the
primary result.

## Instrument identity (ruling, 2026-08-31)
When the asset is an interactive instrument, it must READ as one: a
visually bounded, closed-off object — a border, its own chrome, its
own internal regions — never an article with buttons scattered through
prose. The page's layout differentiates absolutely: the instrument is
the focus; titles, framing, and method notes are contextual and quiet
around it. If a screenshot could be mistaken for a blog post, the
design fails this clause before any other is checked.

And it is designed like a widget, for a cognitively overloaded reader
(Venkat, 2026-08-31): the dominant element is a STATE readable in
under a second — a status, a gauge, a lamp — not a sentence; the first
viewport carries roughly sixty words or fewer; every row offers one
action; sentences are the second disclosure layer, full detail the
third. The test is the airbag designer's: a small space that must
convey everything, instantly, to someone with no attention to spare.

## Language
Part of the experience, not decoration. Recognizable words, plain
verbs, active voice, one job per element, consistent terms, labels
naming what the person recognizes rather than what the system does.

## Failure and correction
Designed before implementation, as applicable: insufficient input ·
uncertainty · refusal · unsupported conclusion · disagreement ·
correction · missing evidence · no useful finding · stale or
superseded information · system failure · privacy limitation ·
incomplete result. "No useful finding" is presented as an honest
outcome, never dressed up.

## Accessibility and cognitive load
Required qualities: plain language, clear hierarchy, one task at a
time, limited visible choices, low working-memory demand, visible
progress when genuinely multi-step, predictable controls, keyboard
access, visible focus, sufficient contrast, semantic structure,
reduced motion, adequate touch targets, no unnecessary animation, no
AI theater, no result dumps. Mechanical signals (choice counts, block
counts, word counts, repeated labels) flag possible problems; judgment
decides. No invented numeric load scores.
