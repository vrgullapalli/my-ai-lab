# Patterns: Purple Cow (Seth Godin)

Concrete techniques from the book, each with when to use it, how, and the trade-off.

## Build the remarkable thing into the product
**When to use**: before any spend on promotion.
**How**: change the offering itself (the can, the packaging, the guarantee, the price model, the service moment) so that using it makes people talk. Dutch Boy changed the can. Aeron changed the chair.
**Trade-offs**: costs engineering and product failures instead of ad dollars. Earlier and repeated spending. Not a cheap shortcut.

## Niche first, product second
**When to use**: launching anything new.
**How**: pick the smallest market you can overwhelm, ideally one that already has otaku. Then design for it. Zespri chose Latino foodies; Krispy Kreme chose donut maniacs; Tracey chose plastic surgeons.
**Trade-offs**: small early numbers. The crossover to the mass market happens only if the niche is dominated and the idea travels.

## Design for the left of the curve
**When to use**: setting product requirements.
**How**: make it remarkable enough to attract early adopters, and simple and demonstrable enough that they can sell it to the majority. Digital cameras: convenience and price, obvious and easy to show.
**Trade-offs**: the majority will still ignore you directly. You depend on peers selling for you, and "they will sell it poorly."

## Run the virus-worthiness checklist
**When to use**: choosing which product ideas to launch.
**How**: score each on ease of spreading, frequency of sneezing, tightness and trust of the group, reputation of the likely promoters, and persistence (fad or legs). Launch the ones that score.
**Trade-offs**: it is a screen, not a guarantee. Some ideaviruses are still luck (the Macarena).

## Advertise only to interested sneezers with influence
**When to use**: any paid placement.
**How**: show up where people are already looking for help (Google text ads next to the search), and make sure the listener is a sneezer. Everything else is the Purple Cow's job.
**Trade-offs**: those moments are rare. Most of the time there is nothing to advertise to.

## Differentiate customers and ignore the rest
**When to use**: allocating product and marketing effort.
**How**: find the most profitable group and the most likely to sneeze. Develop, advertise, and reward for them. The bank's 10 percent daily users held 70 percent of deposits.
**Trade-offs**: you deliberately underserve the center and let slow segments leave.

## The logo-swap test
**When to use**: reviewing any ad, page, post, or deck.
**How**: cover the logo. Could a competitor's name go there? Would anyone request information because of it? If yes to the first or no to the second, it is a brown cow.
**Trade-offs**: none. It costs a minute.

## Take profits from the dying product
**When to use**: an established product whose future cannot be remarkable.
**How**: cut incremental R&D, raise price as far as practical, move the profit into new, more radical products. Godin's orthodox advice for Tide.
**Trade-offs**: accelerates the decline of the old product. Politically hard inside a company built on it.

## Invite users to change behavior
**When to use**: when improving the product for existing behavior yields only increments.
**How**: redesign the interaction so users do something new that makes the product dramatically better. Otis: key in your floor in the lobby, every ride becomes an express.
**Trade-offs**: requires the user to learn something. Works when the payoff is immediate and visible.

## Map the edges, then test one
**When to use**: looking for the Cow with a team.
**How**: for every P (price, packaging, service, speed, selection, and so on), describe the extreme in each direction, mark where competitors sit, and test which extreme you can support financially. Not the free spa; JetBlue's cheap-and-good with a cost structure to match.
**Trade-offs**: there is no plan, only process. Hindsight will make the winner look obvious; foresight will not.

## The slogan as a script
**When to use**: positioning a remarkable offering.
**How**: write the one true boast a fan can repeat in one breath and that brings the friend for the right reason. No "also, and, plus." Leaning Tower, not Pantheon.
**Trade-offs**: purity means leaving things out. If the boast is not true or consistent, the script backfires.

## Sell to the converted (permission asset)
**When to use**: after the first remarkable success.
**How**: collect the addresses of the 20 percent who love you. Make them something super-special. Pearl Jam's seventy-two live albums, all profitable. Start free: an address to write to, and write back.
**Trade-offs**: permission is to alert them about the next Cow, not to sell leftovers. Abuse it and you lose the sneezers.

## The Magic Cycle
**When to use**: running a business on Cows rather than one launch.
**How**: 1) get permission from those you impressed; 2) give the sneezers tools and a story to cross the chasm; 3) let a different team milk the profitable business fast, productize and servicize; 4) reinvest in the next Cow for the same audience, expecting to fail repeatedly.
**Trade-offs**: less predictable and less profitable than the TV era. "But it's all we've got."

## Sit there, don't just do something
**When to use**: when there is no remarkable idea and pressure to "refresh."
**How**: do nothing. Reintroduce classics. Ben & Jerry's; McIntosh's few amplifiers a decade. Take a season off and come back with rested designers.
**Trade-offs**: junior teams get fewer projects. Doing nothing is still worse than doing something great.

## Make the packaging the product
**When to use**: commodities and near-commodities.
**How**: "Bronnify" a variation: make the container, the label, the tin, the box part of the experience and a story to share. Dr. Bronner's, Altoids, Tiffany.
**Trade-offs**: aimed at a specific audience; the mainstream may find it odd, which is part of why it spreads.

## Make a collectible version
**When to use**: a category leader that is practically generic (Band-Aid).
**How**: put characters, editions, or variety into the product so the first user makes others want it. Curad.
**Trade-offs**: fads fade; plan the next variation.

## Change the product, not the ads
**When to use**: sales are down and the reflex is a new slogan or agency.
**How**: ask where the product ends and the hype begins, then redefine what you sell. People buy painted walls, not paint.
**Trade-offs**: means going to the factory, which will resist. Outsource the first run if it does.

## Pick a maverick and get out of the way
**When to use**: someone is charged with creating a Cow.
**How**: no internal reviews, no usability testing against the current product, no committee. Jerry Hirschberg at Nissan; Robyn Waters at Target.
**Trade-offs**: the maverick may fail loudly. Criticism of the project is not criticism of you.

## Prototype in public, cheaply
**When to use**: learning what fans find remarkable when you lack otaku yourself.
**How**: release cheap public prototypes (GM concept cars, the Toy Fair) and measure the response. Not focus groups.
**Trade-offs**: does not work for long-cycle products like airliners.

## Measure everything, fast
**When to use**: always, once a product is live.
**How**: decide what could be measured, what it costs, how fast results arrive. Do more of what spreads, kill what does not. Zara's three-to-four-week cycle.
**Trade-offs**: measurement means admitting what is broken. Mass marketers avoid it for that reason.

## The narrowest possible niche (for a person or a small firm)
**When to use**: a job search or a new practice.
**How**: become the one exceptional choice for one audience. Be remarkable while not looking. References instead of a résumé.
**Trade-offs**: you say no to everyone else. That is the point.

## The eight salt prompts
**When to use**: a product everyone agrees is boring.
**How**: ten product changes for a sliver of the audience; think of the smallest market; outsource if the factory resists; build a permission asset; copy the remarkable player in a duller industry; go one more than the edgiest competitor or do the opposite; do what is "just not done"; ask "why not?" of everything you do not do.
**Trade-offs**: some answers will be too too for most people, and just right for the niche.
