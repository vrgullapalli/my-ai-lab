## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Purple Cow (the new P).** Remarkable means worth making a remark about. "Boring stuff is invisible. It's a brown cow." Build the thing worth noticing into the product or service; do not bolt marketing on at the end. The thesis in one line: "Stop advertising and start innovating." The opposite of remarkable is not bad. It is "very good," the expected outcome nobody mentions.

**Why now.** The TV-industrial complex (average product, TV ads, distribution, profits, more ads) built the big brands for fifty years and is dying. Most people can't buy your product: no money, no time, or no desire. Satisfied customers don't tell friends. Old rule: safe ordinary products plus great marketing. New rule: "Create remarkable products that the right people seek out."

**The curve and the sneezers.** On Moore's idea diffusion curve, the majority ignores you and listens only to peers. The only way in is the left: innovators and early adopters, and among them the sneezers, perceived authorities who spread ideas. Design for them: remarkable enough to attract, simple enough for them to sell onward. "It is useless to advertise to anyone (except interested sneezers with influence)," and only where they are already looking.

**Niche first.** "Don't try to make a product for everybody, because that is a product for nobody." Find the smallest market you can overwhelm, ideally one with otaku (more than a hobby, less than an obsession; hot sauce has it, mustard doesn't). Then make the product. Krispy Kreme, Zespri's golden kiwi, Tracey the publicist to plastic surgeons.

**Virus-worthiness checklist.** Before launch: how smoothly does it spread, how often will people sneeze it, how tight and trusting is the group, how reputable are the promoters, fad or legs? Success is engineered from day one; it is not organic. The Cow is not a cheap shortcut: the money moves from ads to engineering and repeated product failures.

**Safe is risky.** The Cow is rare because people are afraid of criticism. "In a crowded marketplace, fitting in is failing." Nobody gets unanimous praise; that is part of the definition. "You do not equal the project." Boring is always the riskiest strategy. Following the leader fails because the remarkable thing is now taken.

**The process, not a plan.** "There is no plan." Go for the edges: for every P, describe the extreme both ways, mark where competitors sit, and test which extreme you can support financially. JetBlue found cheap-and-good with a cost base to match. Compromise sands the edge off: "a camel is a horse designed by a committee." Pick a maverick and get out of the way.

**The Magic Cycle of the Cow.** 1) Get permission from people you impressed, only to alert them about the next Cow. 2) Give the sneezers tools and a story to cross the chasm. 3) Let a different team milk the profitable business, fast, productize and servicize, don't believe the press. 4) Reinvest in the next Cow for the same audience; fail and fail again. Benefits have a half-life (Starbucks grew for years on its first burst), so milk and build at the same time.

**Measure.** "Every product, every interaction, every policy is either working (persuading sneezers and spreading the word) or not." Mass marketers hate measurement because it means admitting what's broken. Zara turns its line every three or four weeks.

**Marketing is now design.** The product is the marketing (Dutch Boy's can, the Aeron chair, Tiffany's box, 1-800-COLLECT). The marketer must sit where the product is invented. A slogan is a script for the sneezer: true, consistent, one breath, no "also, and, plus." Remarkable comes from otaku (Howard Schultz and coffee) or from learning to project, by empathy or by launching, measuring, and repeating with cheap public prototypes.

**Fast tests.** Logo-swap: cover the logo on any ad; if a competitor's fits, kill it. Parody: if nobody could parody you, you are boring. "So what?": awareness that changes no behavior is nothing. Cheap: only if you can leap the cost structure; otherwise it is the last refuge of someone out of ideas. Outrageous: only with a purpose, built into the product.

**Doing nothing.** With no remarkable idea, "sit there, don't just do something." A new slogan instead of a new product is a compromise worse than nothing.

**The proof.** Of Interbrand's top 100 brands (2002), seventy were built when TV was cheap; of the rest, half grew on word of mouth; only AOL bought its way in during the last decade of media excess. "Do you want to grow? If you do, you need to embrace the Cow."
