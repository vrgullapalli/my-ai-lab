# Cheatsheet: Purple Cow (Seth Godin)

One page of decisions, thresholds, and tells. Use it while judging an offering, a launch, or a piece of promotion.

## Decision rules

| When | Do | Because |
|---|---|---|
| About to spend on ads, a deck, a trade show, a campaign | Stop. Spend the time with engineers and customers on a blank sheet | Ads reach innovators who are bored and the majority who won't listen |
| The offering is "very good" | Treat it as invisible; find the attribute to push to "most" | The opposite of remarkable is very good, not bad |
| A competitor's logo would fit your ad | Kill the ad | Nobody will remember it or pass it on |
| Someone reports awareness, reach, impressions | Ask "so what?"; count sneezes and sales | Kmart has plenty of awareness |
| Choosing a target market | Pick the smallest niche you can overwhelm, with otaku already present | Everybody products are taken; hot sauce is a business, mustard is not |
| Choosing which product ideas to launch | Run the virus-worthiness checklist; launch the scorers | Success is engineered from day one, not organic |
| Tempted to target the center of the curve | Design for early adopters and give them an easy story | The majority listens to peers, never to you |
| Deciding whether to advertise at all | Only to interested sneezers with influence, where they are already looking | Everything else is waste with a huge denominator |
| A product cannot be remarkable again | Cut its R&D, raise price, fund the next Cow | Investing in a dying product wastes the profits you still have |
| No remarkable idea and pressure to "refresh" | Do nothing; reintroduce classics | Mediocre refreshes short-circuit your remaining fans |
| Considering competing on price | Only if you can change how it is made and leap 30 to 50 percent | Incremental price wars are won by IKEA and Wal-Mart, not you |
| Tempted to be shocking | Only if the edge has a purpose and lives in the product | Outrageous without purpose is desperation |
| A committee wants to review the Cow | Refuse; pick a maverick and leave them alone | Committees sand the edges off |
| Your boss wants focus groups to prove it | Skip them; prototype cheaply in public | If the focus group likes it, they're probably wrong |
| Told to pick one product that has to work | Launch ten small bets instead | Things that have to work rarely do |
| The first Cow is profitable | Hand it to a milking team and start the next Cow now | Benefits have a half-life; coasting is how empires stall |
| A market where sneezers have stopped looking | Leave, or commit to a series of Cows | An attention blockade cannot be bought through |
| Looking for a job or a first client | Be remarkable now, in the narrowest niche; references, not a résumé | The pile at Monster is where you don't want to be |

## The four-step cycle (run it as a loop)
1. Get permission from people you impressed. Only to alert them about the next Cow.
2. Give the sneezers tools and a story to cross the chasm for you.
3. Let a different team milk the profitable business. Fast. Don't believe the press.
4. Reinvest in the next Cow for the same audience. Expect to fail repeatedly.

## Virus-worthiness checklist (score before launch)
- How smooth and easy is it to spread?
- How often will people sneeze it?
- How tight is the group; do they talk and believe each other?
- How reputable are the likely promoters?
- Fad or legs?

## Edge map (the process; there is no plan)
For each P (price, packaging, speed, service, selection, size, voice): name the extreme both ways, mark where competitors sit, test which extreme you can support financially. Limits to test: cheapest, fastest, slowest, hottest, coldest, easiest, most efficient, loudest, most hated, the copycat, the outsider, hardest, oldest, newest.

## Thresholds and rules of thumb the author commits to
- Ten launches at $10 million beat one at $100 million on TV.
- Get the addresses of the 20 percent who love you.
- If you could build a competitor with costs 30 percent lower, someone will.
- Southwest and JetBlue's 50 percent cost advantage is unbeatable by hub carriers.
- Only about six of the top 100 brands (2002) were built purely the old way, and only one of those (AOL) in the last ten years of media spend.
- Zara turns its line every three to four weeks; measure that fast if you can.
- Google home page: a fan counts the words; two buttons was the win. Yahoo! has 500 words.

## Tells and smells
- **A committee wrote it** (the BearingPoint ad): nobody will remember it.
- **"A little bit cheaper / better / easier"**: invisible even to a listener.
- **"Everyone likes it"**: then it is boring. Remarkable means some people won't.
- **No one has ever parodied you**: you are L.L. Bean, not J. Peterman.
- **The parking lot is full and nothing is unusual any more**: you are milking, not building (Stew Leonard's).
- **"That's just not done in our industry"**: a candidate for the Cow.
- **"We don't do that" with no reason**: fear, inertia, or nobody asked "why not?"
- **The chocolate is worse than the coffee**: no otaku on that product line.
- **A new slogan instead of a new product**: a budget compromise, worse than nothing.
- **Front-loaded launch budget**: the product will be dead before sneezers reach the majority.

## Trade-off matrix: where to put the next dollar

| Option | Reaches sneezers | Spreads on its own | Cost timing | Godin's verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mass media campaign | No | No | All up front, one shot | Dead; 6 percent of top brands, one in ten years |
| Targeted ad where people already search | Yes, sometimes | Weak | Pay per moment | Fine when the moment exists; rare |
| Product change (the can, the chair, the guarantee) | Yes | Yes | Earlier, repeated, with failures | The only growth strategy left |
| Permission asset plus special offer to the 20 percent | Yes | Yes | Low per sale | Step one of the cycle; start today |
| Cheap prototype in public | Yes | Learns fast | Small, repeated | The science of projecting |
| Price cut | Everyone | Briefly | Ongoing | Lazy, unless a structural leap |
