# Glossary: Purple Cow (Seth Godin)

Terms as Godin uses them. Chapter numbers refer to this skill's chapter files, not the book's short sections.

**Attention blockade**: a market where the sneezers have stopped looking, so nothing new can spread; classical music is the case (Ch 4)

**Awareness**: being known; not the point, since attention that changes no behavior sells nothing ("Kmart has plenty of awareness. So what?") (Ch 3)

**Before, During, and After Advertising**: the three eras of how products spread: word of mouth, then mass media, then word of mouth again at network speed (Ch 1)

**Boring**: not standing out; "always the most risky strategy," except when boring is itself remarkable (Ch 5)

**Brown cow**: a good product nobody notices because it looks like every other one (Ch 1)

**Catch-22 of the Cow**: in bad times you can't afford to be remarkable; in good times you don't need to be (Ch 5)

**Cheap**: an edge that always gains share but invites a price war; "the last refuge" of someone out of ideas, unless it is a quantum leap in how the thing is made (Ch 10)

**Cheating**: what entrenched competitors call winners who don't play by the old rules: JetBlue, Vanguard, Google, HBO (Ch 4)

**Compromise**: sanding down edges to gain buy-in; a camel is a horse designed by a committee; vanilla is a compromise flavor (Ch 8)

**Cross the chasm**: Moore's term for moving from early adopters to the majority; in the Cow it is the sneezers' job, with your tools and story (Ch 4, Ch 9)

**Distinct or extinct**: the choice every business faces; the three music-industry niche companies (Ch 12)

**Early adopters**: people who benefit from a new product and want to keep their edge; the door into the curve (Ch 4)

**Early and late majority**: the big profitable group that ignores you and listens only to peers (Ch 4)

**Edges**: the extremes on any P (cheapest, fastest, most exclusive); the process is to map them and test which you can support (Ch 8, Ch 12)

**Explore the limits**: the prompt to test every "most": cheapest, slowest, loudest, most hated (Ch 12)

**Follow the leader**: the flock strategy of copying a breakthrough; fails because the remarkable thing is now taken and the flock rotates leaders (Ch 2, Ch 5)

**Half-life of benefits**: the upside of a Cow lasts after the Cow stops being remarkable; Starbucks grew on its first burst (Ch 6)

**Hurricane advertising**: untargeted ads that touch everyone the same way; occasionally works, so rarely it is wasteful (Ch 4)

**Idea diffusion curve**: Moore's curve: innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards (Ch 4)

**Ideavirus**: an idea that spreads; Godin's earlier book; "ideas that spread, win" (Ch 4)

**Innovators**: people who like having something first; may not need it (Ch 4)

**Laggards**: the true adapters; change only when the old thing is gone (Ch 4)

**Law of large numbers**: big audiences come with fractions that have huge denominators; 300 million banners, under $500 of sales (Ch 4)

**Logo-swap test**: if a competitor's logo fits your ad, nobody could tell; the Wall Street Journal experiment (Ch 3)

**Magic Cycle of the Cow**: get permission, arm the sneezers, let a different team milk it, reinvest in the next Cow (Ch 9)

**Making news versus making the news**: having something worth reporting versus forcing publicity (Ch 12)

**Milk the Cow**: extend and profit from a remarkable success as long as possible, fast, while building the next one (Ch 6, Ch 9)

**Niche**: a segment small enough that a few sneezers reach critical mass; find it first, then make the product (Ch 4, Ch 8)

**Otaku**: Japanese: more than a hobby, less than an obsession; the itch that makes someone drive across town; the sneezers you seek; hot sauce has it, mustard does not (Ch 8, Ch 9)

**Outrageous**: not the same as remarkable; only counts with a purpose, built into the product (Ch 10)

**Parody paradox**: showing up in a parody proves you are unique; the same word of mouth that spreads you also snickers at you (Ch 7)

**Permission asset**: the list of people who asked to hear about your next Cow; not a list to spam or squeeze (Ch 7, Ch 9)

**Post-consumption consumer**: has what they need, wants little, and is too busy to research what you made (Ch 1)

**Projecting**: getting inside the heads of people who care deeply about a product you do not; the art (empathy) and the science (launch, measure, learn, repeat) (Ch 9)

**Purple Cow**: the new P: remarkable; worth noticing, exceptional, new, interesting; built into the offering, not added on (Ch 1)

**Remarkable**: worth making a remark about; its opposite is not bad but "very good" (Ch 1, Ch 7)

**Safe is risky**: in a crowded market, fitting in is failing; not standing out is being invisible (Ch 5)

**Slogan as script**: a true, consistent boast a sneezer can repeat to a friend; Tiffany's blue box is a slogan without words (Ch 8)

**Sneezer**: a perceived authority who tells colleagues, friends, or admirers about a product; the spreading agent of an ideavirus (Ch 4)

**Squeeze play**: marketers can't get the word out because jaded consumers refuse attention and rely on trusted suppliers and friends instead (Ch 1)

**Takeaway marker**: the symbol the book uses for its action items; collected in this skill's cheatsheet (Ch 3)

**TV-industrial complex**: the cycle of average product, TV ads, distribution, profits, more ads; built the big brands for fifty years and is dying (Ch 1, Ch 2)

**Very good**: the everyday, expected outcome; invisible; the opposite of remarkable (Ch 7)

**Virus-worthiness**: how smoothly an idea spreads, how often it is sneezed, how tight and trusting the group is, how reputable the promoters, how persistent the idea (Ch 4)

**Will and the way**: ideas are plentiful; the will to execute is what is missing (Ch 3)
