# Chapter 4: Getting In: the Curve, the Sneezers, and Who Is Listening (book pages 28–44)

Covers the sections: Getting In · Ideas That Spread, Win · The Big Misunderstanding · Who's Listening? · Cheating · Who Cares? · Not All Customers Are the Same · The Law of Large Numbers · Case Study: Chip Conley

## Core Idea
Sales come from the right side of the idea diffusion curve, but the right side ignores you. The only door in is the left: innovators and early adopters, and among them the sneezers who spread ideas. So design for a niche, make it remarkable to that niche, and give the sneezers an idea that is easy to pass on.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The idea diffusion curve** (Moore): innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards. "The vast majority of the curve ignores you. Every time." The majority listens to experienced peers, not to you, and often not even to the innovators.
  - How: "design a product that is remarkable enough to attract the early adopters, but is flexible enough and attractive enough that those adopters will have an easy time spreading the idea to the rest of the curve."
- **Ideavirus and sneezers**: "Ideas that spread are more likely to succeed than those that don't. I call ideas that spread, ideaviruses. Sneezers are the key spreading agents." Sneezers are perceived authorities who tell colleagues, friends, or admirers. Early adopters who are not sneezers are "a dead end."
- **Niche first**: "Don't try to make a product for everybody, because that is a product for nobody." Segment a chunk of the mainstream and build an ideavirus so focused it overwhelms that slice. Then, "if you're good and you're lucky," it migrates to the masses.
- **The virus-worthiness checklist** (put every new product through it): How smooth and easy is it to spread? How often will people sneeze it? How tightly knit is the group, do they talk, do they believe each other? How reputable are the likely promoters? How persistent is it, a fad or an idea with legs?
- **The big idea on advertising**: "It is useless to advertise to anyone (except interested sneezers with influence)." Advertise when they are actually looking for help, in a place where they will find you. Google's text ads work because they are contextually relevant and shown to people likely to act.
- **Differentiate your customers**: "Find the group that's most profitable. Find the group that's most likely to sneeze. Figure out how to develop/advertise/reward either group. Ignore the rest." Cater to the customers you would choose if you could choose.
- **The law of large numbers**: large audiences "are almost always accompanied by fractions with huge denominators." 300 million banner ads for $600 sold under $500 of merchandise.

## Key Concepts
- **Innovators**: like having something first. May not need it.
- **Early adopters**: benefit from the new thing and want to keep their edge. Not "early adapters."
- **Early and late majority**: want protocols, systems, safety. Very good at ignoring you.
- **Laggards**: the real adapters. Change only when the old thing is gone.
- **Sneezer**: a perceived authority who spreads the idea. Often an early adopter, not always.
- **The big misunderstanding**: readers of Chasm, Tipping Point, and Ideavirus concluded spread is organic and automatic. Wrong. "The vast majority of product success stories are engineered from the first day." Marketing now means "designing the thing to be virus-worthy in the first place."
- **Cheating**: JetBlue, Starbucks, Vanguard, Amazon, Google, Wendy's, Ducati, HBO. To nervous competitors they look like they are not playing by the rules. "Why aren't you cheating?"
- **Attention blockade**: classical music. Sneezers have all the music they will ever buy, so the whole market stopped. Naxos won by selling good cheap versions of known works. Sony Classical could not.
- **Hurricane advertising**: untargeted, touching everyone the same way. Sometimes it skips the left-to-right work. "Sometimes is pretty rare, so rare that it's wasteful."

## Mental Models
- Think of digital cameras: gadget-heads bought first, makers fixed usability and quality, early adopters sold their friends. Convenience and price were "obvious, easy to talk about, easy to demonstrate." No ad campaign did that.
- Use the bank case: 10 percent of customers used online banking daily. A consultant would cut the service. That 10 percent held about 70 percent of deposits. The valuable slice is often at one edge, not the center.
- When a market has stopped listening, "the smartest plan is usually to leave. Plan B is to have the insight and guts to go after a series of Purple Cows."
- The Purple Cow is not a cheap shortcut. The money moves from ads to "repeated engineering expenses and product failures." Earlier, and more often.

## Anti-patterns
- **Skipping the left of the curve for the juicy center**: it does not work any more.
- **"A little bit cheaper, a little bit better, a little bit easier"**: a waste of time even with a listener.
- **Waiting for the virus to happen**: sitting back and expecting consumers to do your job.
- **Believing in the large number**: one in a thousand Oscar viewers is a fantasy with a huge denominator.

## Worked Example
Chip Conley buys the Phoenix, a motel in one of the worst neighborhoods in downtown San Francisco, for next to nothing. "Hardly anyone" would choose to stay there, which is fine for a few dozen rooms. He paints it funky colors, puts hip magazines in rooms, has an artist paint the inside of the pool, invites rising rock stars to stay. Within months it is the rock-and-roll motel of San Francisco. "People looking for it found it." Takeaway: list competitors who are not trying to be everything to everyone. Pick one underserved niche to dominate. Consider launching a product that competes with your own and appeals only to that niche.

## Key Takeaways
1. Design for the left of the curve. The right side will only hear it from peers.
2. Find the sneezers. Early adopters who do not spread are a dead end.
3. Choose a niche you can overwhelm. Everybody products are all taken.
4. Run the virus-worthiness checklist before launch, not after.
5. Advertise only to interested sneezers with influence, where they are already looking.
6. Measure the fraction, not the audience.
