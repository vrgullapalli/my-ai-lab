# Chapter 6: Projections, Profits, Measurement, and the Benefits of Being the Cow (book pages 55–66)

Covers the sections: Projections, Profits, and the Purple Cow · Case Study: The Best Baker in the World · Mass Marketers Hate to Measure · Case Study: Logitech · Who Wins in the World of the Cow · Case Study: A New Kind of Kiwi · The Benefits of Being the Cow · Case Study: The Italian Butcher · Wall Street and the Cow

## Core Idea
Mass marketing forces boring products and scary budgets that leave no time for an idea to spread. The Cow's economics are the reverse: put the money into the product, measure everything, aim at a niche, and when it works, milk it while you build the next one.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The two-part catch-22 of mass marketing**:
  - Part one, boring products: companies built around mass marketing "round the edges, smooth out the differentiating features," make spicy food less spicy and insanely great service a little less great, because ads for everyone need a product for everyone. The ads reach innovators (bored, ignore it) and the majority (do not listen to ads for new products).
  - Part two, scary budgets: a national rollout costs a hundred times a local one. The ads must work fast. You get few shots, so you make safe bets, and the budget burns out before the idea can move along the curve. The dot-com graveyard: great products, capital spent on mass marketing "that came too soon and disappeared before the idea could spread."
  - Takeaway: "What would happen if you gave the marketing budget for your next three products to the designers?"
- **Measure**: "Every product, every interaction, every policy is either working (persuading sneezers and spreading the word) or not." Mass marketers resist measurement because it means admitting what is broken. Zara changes its line every three or four weeks by watching what works. Takeaway: "What could you measure? What would that cost? How fast could you get the results? If you can afford it, try it. 'If you measure it, it will improve.'"
- **Milk the Cow and build the next one**: once you have something remarkable, do two contradictory things at once. "Milk the Cow for everything it's worth." And "create an environment where you are likely to invent a new Purple Cow in time to replace the first one when its benefits inevitably trail off."
- **Who wins**: losers are giant brands with big factories, quarterly targets, inertia, and low thresholds for perceived risk. Winners are mid-sized and smaller companies with nothing to lose and much to gain from changing the rules (with exceptions both ways).

## Key Concepts
- **Half-life of benefits**: "You don't have to be remarkable all the time to enjoy the upside." Starbucks was remarkable, is now boring, and grew to thousands of stores on that first burst. Maxwell House played safe and is stuck where it was a decade ago.
- **The temptation to coast**: Palm, Yahoo!, AOL, Marriott, Marvel. Breakthrough, empire, then no more risk. It used to be possible to coast for decades (Disney, Milton Berle). Not now.
- **The IPO lesson**: companies that went public in the boom had one thing in common, a proven Purple Cow. Then almost all took profits, "streamlined and mechanized and milked their Cow," and forgot to build the next.
- **Logitech is in the fashion business**: the guts of the mouse rarely change; functionality and style change constantly. Frequent users proselytize. "Logitech doesn't crave more advertising. They crave more remarkable products."
- **Zespri golden kiwi**: aimed at Latino foodies through upscale Latino groceries, no advertising, over $100 million in sales, and most people have never seen one. Niche of risk-taking sneezers first, then cross over.
- **Rewards rise as fewer try**: as the world gets turbulent, more people seek safety, so fewer attempt the Cow while a restless minority is more willing than ever to switch. "The rewards for being remarkable continue to increase."

## Mental Models
- Compare the Blair Witch or Greek Wedding launch to a $20 million studio campaign. Make the remarkable film, let the people who see every movie find it, let the word spread.
- Think of the Poilane bakery: interviewed eight thousand bakers, pioneered organic flour in France, refused to bake baguettes, hired apprentices instead of bakers with bad habits. Rejected as too daring, then every fancy Paris restaurant served the bread. Over $10 million of bread a year, shipped worldwide.
- Think of Dario Cecchini, the one famous (and rich) butcher among thousands in Italy. Quotes Dante, held a mock funeral with a casket for bone-in steak when the EU banned it. "Is his meat that much better? Probably not." He made buying meat an intellectual and political event.

## Anti-patterns
- **Front-loading the budget** so the product is dead before sneezers reach the majority.
- **Refusing to measure** because the data would make the job harder.
- **Coasting on the first Cow**: taking profits, failing to reinvest, treating every new chance as a threat to what you earned.

## Worked Example
Sam Adams took a big slice from Budweiser. Hard Manufacturing's $3,000 Doernbecher crib opened a new hospital-crib segment. Yamaha's electric piano took share from traditional piano leaders. Vanguard's low-cost funds keep chipping at Fidelity. BIC lost share to Japanese pens that were "remarkably fun to write with," exactly as BIC had taken the market from fountain pens a generation earlier. Same pattern, every time: the remarkable entrant beats the entrenched one, and then becomes the one to beat.

## Key Takeaways
1. Move the budget from media to the product. Ask what a world-class designer would cost.
2. Measure every product, interaction, and policy. Do more of what spreads, kill what does not.
3. Aim at a niche of sneezers first. Zespri sold $100 million without ads.
4. When it works, milk it hard and fast, and start the next Cow at the same time.
5. The upside has a half-life. Coasting is how empires stall.
