# Chapter 1: The New P (book pages 1–7)

Covers the sections: Not Enough Ps · The New P · Boldfaced Words and Gutsy Assertions · Before, During, and After · The Greatest Thing Since Sliced Bread · Did You Notice the Revolution?

## Core Idea
The classic marketing Ps (product, pricing, promotion, positioning, publicity, packaging, pass-along, permission) are no longer enough. A new P, the Purple Cow, matters most: the offering itself must be remarkable, or it is invisible.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Purple Cow**: "Something remarkable is worth talking about. Worth noticing. Exceptional. New. Interesting. It's a Purple Cow. Boring stuff is invisible. It's a brown cow."
  - When to use: any time you are about to spend on promotion for something that is merely fine.
  - How: build the thing worth noticing into the product or service. Do not bolt marketing on at the end.
- **Before, During, and After Advertising**: three eras of how products spread.
  - Before: word of mouth. The best vegetable seller had a reputation and a crowded booth.
  - During: TV and mass media. "If you advertised directly to the consumer (every consumer), sales would go up."
  - After: back to word of mouth, but networks let remarkable ideas "diffuse through segments of the population at rocket speed."
- **Stop advertising and start innovating**: the book's one-line thesis.

## Key Concepts
- **Remarkable**: worth making a remark about. Not "good," not "well made." Talked about.
- **Brown cow**: a perfectly good product that nobody notices because it looks like every other one.
- **TV-industrial complex**: "the symbiotic relationship between consumer demand, TV advertising, and ever-growing companies that were built around investments in ever-increasing marketing expenditures."
- **Post-consumption consumer**: "out of things to buy. We have what we need, we want very little, and we're too busy."
- **The marketing department, old model**: "takes a nearly finished product or service and spends money to communicate its special benefits to a target audience. This approach no longer works."

## Mental Models
- Think of the cows in France: beautiful for twenty minutes, then boring. Any offering, however good, becomes invisible once people have seen its kind before.
- Treat the earlier books as parts of one picture. Peters (passion), Peppers and Rogers (keep customers, four kinds of people), Moore (idea diffusion curve), Gladwell (tipping point), Godin (ideavirus, permission). Companies treated them as fads. They are the whole strategy now.
- Sliced bread failed for twenty years until Wonder marketed it. That was the start of the advertising age. The lesson of the book is that the age is over.

## Anti-patterns
- **Marketing as a last-minute add-on**: if the offering is not remarkable, no amount of promotion saves it.
- **Treating new marketing ideas as novelties**: "New media has become a synonym for no budget." A department of one is not a strategy.
- **Ps out of whack with each other**: puréed meals for seniors that taste like baby food. The message blurs.

## Worked Example
Godin's family drives through France. Hundreds of storybook cows by the highway. Everyone stares. Twenty minutes later nobody looks. "The new cows were just like the old cows, and what once was amazing was now common. Worse than common. It was boring." A purple cow would have been interesting, at least for a while. That "for a while" is also part of the model: remarkable wears off.

## Key Takeaways
1. If the offering itself is not remarkable, it is invisible. Fix the offering before you fix the message.
2. We are in the After Advertising era. Word of mouth is back, but it now moves fast through networks.
3. The old Ps are a checklist for a factory. The Purple Cow is a design requirement.
4. "Stop advertising and start innovating."
