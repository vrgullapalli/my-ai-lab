# Chapter 3: Awareness Is Not the Point (book pages 21–27)

Covers the sections: Why The Wall Street Journal Annoys Me So Much · Awareness Is Not the Point · The Will and the Way · Case Study: Going Up? · Case Study: What Should Tide Do?

## Core Idea
Ads that get attention but change no behavior are waste. What is missing is not ideas but the will to execute remarkable ones. Two cases show the two moves: change how users behave so the product works far better (Otis), or take the profits from a product that will never be remarkable again and fund something new (Tide).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The logo-swap test**: "If you took 90 percent of these ads and swapped the logos around, no one could tell." Godin folded the logo under on a Wall Street Journal full-page ad and asked readers to name the company. No idea. Had anyone ever requested information because of one? No.
  - When to use: before approving any ad, page, or post. If a competitor's name fits, it is a brown cow.
- **Awareness is not the point**: Sergio Zyman on two of the most loved Coke commercials ever ("I'd like to teach the world to sing," "Mean Joe Greene"): they "sold not one more bottle of Coke." His line: "Kmart has plenty of awareness. So what?"
- **The Will and the Way**: "what's missing isn't the ideas. It's the will to execute them." The book's goal is to make it "safer to be risky."
- **Invite users to change behavior** (Otis): "Instead of trying to use your technology and expertise to make a better product for your users' standard behavior, experiment with inviting the users to change their behavior to make the product work dramatically better."
- **Take profits from the dying product** (Tide): "If a product's future is unlikely to be remarkable... take profits and reinvest them in building something new."

## Key Concepts
- **Committee ad**: the BearingPoint (formerly KPMG Consulting) full page. "A committee wrote this ad. A committee approved it. No one will remember it."
- **Measurable impact**: an ad that makes people "sit up, take notice, and tell their colleagues." Possible, rare, and still not enough on its own.
- **The takeaway marker**: the book marks its action items with a symbol. This skill lists them under Key Takeaways and in the cheatsheet.

## Mental Models
- Use "So what?" as a gate on any awareness number. Reach, impressions, and views are not the outcome.
- Think of Otis's pre-sort: key in your floor in the lobby, get assigned an elevator, every ride becomes an express. Buildings need fewer elevators and can be taller. Now "Otis gets the benefit of the doubt" no matter how many lunches competitors buy.
- Think of Tide's chemists. Millions a year to make the best detergent slightly better, in a category where nobody cares about laundry any more and a third of sales run through one chain.

## Anti-patterns
- **Buying a house-in-Buffalo-sized ad** for a dull product from a dull company.
- **Defending TV with old success stories**: the old guard's proof is from a different market.
- **Incremental R&D on a product whose future cannot be remarkable**: the money is better spent on the next Cow.

## Worked Example
What should Tide do? Orthodox Purple Cow thinking, in Godin's words: "take the profits while they're still there. Cut research spending, raise the price as much as is practical, and put the incremental profits into the creation of ever more radical and interesting new products. If the current R&D isn't likely to generate a noteworthy payoff, what's the point?" The test: can you imagine a future in which people are once again fascinated by this product? If not, the game has changed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run the logo-swap test on everything you publish.
2. Ask "so what?" of every awareness metric.
3. Ideas are not scarce. Will is. Decide, then act.
4. Look for ways users could change their behavior that make your offering dramatically better.
5. Stop feeding a product that cannot be remarkable again. Fund the next one.
