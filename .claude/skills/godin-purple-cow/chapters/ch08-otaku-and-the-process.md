# Chapter 8: Otaku, the Process, and the Problem with Compromise (book pages 79–93)

Covers the sections: In Search of Otaku · Case Study: How Dutch Boy Stirred Up the Paint Business · Case Study: Krispy Kreme · The Process and the Plan · The Power of a Slogan · Case Study: The Häagen-Dazs in Bronxville · Sell What People Are Buying (and Talking About!) · The Problem with Compromise · Case Study: Motorola and Nokia

## Core Idea
Find the market where people already have otaku, an itch stronger than a hobby, and make something remarkable for them. There is no plan for a Cow, but there is a process: map your edges and your competitors' on every P, then test which edge you can support. Compromise sands the edge off and kills it.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Otaku**: "more than a hobby but a little less than an obsession." The desire that makes someone drive across town for a new ramen shop. "Consumers with otaku are the sneezers you seek." Some markets have more otaku than others: hot sauce is a business (Dave's Insanity, Blair's After Death), mustard is not, because nobody orders mustard by mail. "Smart businesses target markets where there's already otaku." Takeaway: go to a science fiction convention; do you appeal to an audience that wacky and wonderful, and how could you create one? (Jeep did. So did Fast Company and Longaberger baskets.)
- **The process, not the plan**: "There is no plan." What there is: "Go for the edges. Challenge yourself and your team to describe what those edges are (not that you'd actually go there), and then test which edge is most likely to deliver the marketing and financial results you seek. By reviewing every other P, your pricing, your packaging, and so forth, you sketch out where your edges are... and where your competition is." A free spa is remarkable but unsupported; JetBlue reached the edge of service and pricing with a model that was also profitable.
- **The slogan as script**: "A slogan that accurately conveys the essence of your Purple Cow is a script. A script for the sneezer to use when she talks with her friends." Tiffany's blue box is a slogan without words. The Leaning Tower of Pisa gets a hundred times the visitors of the Pantheon because there is no "also, and, or plus." Takeaway: "Do you have a slogan or positioning statement or remarkable boast that's actually true? Is it consistent? Is it worth passing on?"
- **Sell what people are buying**: "It's a lot easier to sell something that people are already in the mood to buy." Start with a problem the customer knows he has, make the solution remarkable enough that early adopters respond, then promote it where the sneezers are paying attention. Altoids: young adults who did not smoke needed something for their fingers and mouths at work; a tin that begged to be shared; urban ads with edgy imagery. One of the most profitable candy launches ever.
- **The problem with compromise**: "A camel is a horse designed by a committee." Vanilla is a compromise flavor; habanero pecan is not. "The real growth comes with products that annoy, offend, don't appeal, are too expensive, too cheap, too heavy, too complicated, too simple, too something." Takeaway: "If someone in your organization is charged with creating a new Purple Cow, leave them alone! Don't use internal reviews and usability testing... pick the right maverick and get out of the way."
- **Niche first, then the product** (Krispy Kreme): "find the market niche first, and then make the remarkable product, not the other way around."

## Key Concepts
- **Change the product, not the ads** (Dutch Boy): paint cans were heavy, hard to open, close, and pour from, and everyone assumed there was a reason. There was not. An easy-pour jug raised sales, distribution, and retail price. "People don't buy paint; they buy painted walls." Takeaway: "Where does your product end and marketing hype begin? Can you redefine what you sell?"
- **Krispy Kreme's two phases**: give away thousands of donuts on opening so the people who know the legend show up and drag friends. Then dominate the conversation by putting the product in gas stations, coffee shops, and delis, so the lazy can stumble on it and some become the next wave of otaku.
- **The business card with a home phone number** (Häagen-Dazs, Bronxville): the owner's card says call me at home. Customers notice, staff notice the customers noticing. It works because it is unusual. Takeaway: in an intangibles business, your card is a big part of what you sell; give everyone a second card that actually sells them.
- **The boring slot is filled**: the product built to appeal to the largest audience already exists and its innocuousness is its asset. You cannot market yourself as "more bland than the leading brand."
- **The Cow has left the room** (Motorola and Nokia): phones became boring once everyone had one. Smaller no longer excites. Attempts: a $21,000 Vertu, disposable phones, camera phones. It may be a long time before the market pays attention again.
- **Bootstrappers upend incumbents** because the dominant players are "the last places you'll find empowered mavericks." Their dependence on the middle is the seed of their destruction.

## Mental Models
- Rank markets by otaku before size. Small and passionate beats large and indifferent.
- Sketch the edge map: for each P (price, packaging, service, speed, selection, and so on), where is the extreme, who is already there, and which extreme could you support financially?
- Test the slogan as a hand-off: could a fan repeat it to a friend in one breath, and would the friend come for the right reason?

## Anti-patterns
- **Butterball pot pies on the Food Channel**: an unremarkable retro product, advertised to people not in the market for it, in a medium where few sneezers are watching. "It doesn't solve anyone's problem except for Butterball's."
- **Internal review and usability testing** used to check whether the new thing is as good as what you have. That is how the edges get sanded.
- **Assuming the can has a reason**: most "that's how it's done" has no reason at all.

## Worked Example
Edge mapping, as Godin frames it. Take a spa. Edges on price: free at one end, ultra-luxury at the other. Free is remarkable but "without a financial model that supports that, it's not clear that you'd last very long." So ask which edge you can support. JetBlue found the edge of both service and price with a cost structure that stayed profitable. Archie McPhee did it in retail with selection. Starbucks did it by redefining what a cup of coffee meant. "It's not the tactics or the plan that joins the Purple Cow products together. It's the process organizations use to discover... the fringes that make their products remarkable."

## Key Takeaways
1. Target markets that already have otaku. Hot sauce, not mustard.
2. Map your edges and your competitors' on every P. Pick the edge you can afford.
3. Write a slogan that is a true, consistent script a sneezer can pass on.
4. Start with a problem people know they have, then make the solution remarkable, then show it where sneezers look.
5. Keep committees away from the Cow. Pick a maverick and get out of the way.
6. Find the niche first, then build the product for it.
