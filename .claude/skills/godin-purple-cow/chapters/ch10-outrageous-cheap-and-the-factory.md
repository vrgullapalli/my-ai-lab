# Chapter 10: Outrageous, Cheap, and the Factory Objection (book pages 102–109)

Covers the sections: Do You Have to Be Outrageous to Be Remarkable? · Case Study: McDonald's France · But What About the Factory? · The Problem with Cheap · Case Study: What Should Hallmark.com Do?

## Core Idea
Outrageous is not the same as remarkable; the edge has to have a purpose and live in the product. The biggest objection, the sunk investment in the factory, is answered by spending time with engineers and customers instead of on another ad campaign. Cheap is a lazy edge unless it is a quantum leap. Hallmark shows how to turn permission plus sneezers into a spreading offer.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Outrageous versus remarkable**: "Outrageous is not always remarkable. It's certainly not required. Sometimes outrageous is just annoying." Being scandalous "is not a strategy; it's desperation. The outrageousness needs to have a purpose, and it needs to be built into the product." Hooters ("Delightfully tacky, yet unrefined") is just outrageous enough for its audience without offending it; not everyone likes it, which is part of why it works. "It's not about the way you say it, it's what you say." Takeaway: you are probably too shy, not too outrageous. Practice being at the edge, sparingly.
- **Tell the truth** (McDonald's France): the subsidiary published a report urging the French not to eat fast food more than once a week. Uproar. Maybe honesty reaches a far larger audience than more-more-more. Takeaway: "What would happen if you told the truth?"
- **The factory objection, answered**: the company grew on the TV-industrial complex, invested in people, policies, distribution, and a factory, and now nothing works. The instinct is to fix the marketing and protect the infrastructure. Godin: "if you don't have time to do it right, what makes you think you'll have time to do it over?" Before another campaign, trade show, or sales conference, "spend some time with your engineers and your customers. Challenge your people to start with a blank sheet of paper... If they weren't afraid of failing, what's the most audacious thing they'd try?"
- **The problem with cheap**: cheap always gains share for repeat purchases, but "once you start, your competitor will likely play the same game." IKEA and Wal-Mart can win a price war; can you? "Cheap is the last refuge of a product developer or marketer who is out of great ideas." The exception is the quantum leap: radically redefine how the product is made or delivered and leapfrog everyone's pricing (Southwest and JetBlue's 50 percent cost advantage; Motel 6, clean and cheap; IKEA, cheap but not cheapo). Takeaway: "If you could build a competitor that had costs that were 30 percent lower than yours, could you do it? If you could, why don't you?"

## Key Concepts
- **Best Buy's harder path**: Brad Anderson: "Instead of selling what we wanted to sell, we sold what people wanted us to sell, and then figured out how to make money doing it... every time, that path was the right path." The remarkable store took Best Buy from $250 million to over $23 billion. It looked slower and turned out faster and cheaper than boring ads.
- **Remarkable is not always the biggest machine**: "It can be the way you answer the phone, launch a new brand, or price a revision to your software." Doing the unsafe thing every time you can is how you learn to project.
- **Three forces at Hallmark**: permission (people come to the site on their own), a self-selected group with a problem (Gold Crown Club members want more points), and sneezers (they send a ton of cards and the recipients trust them because the sender gains nothing).

## Mental Models
- Use the purpose test on any edge: does the extreme serve the audience and live in the product, or is it noise bolted onto an ordinary thing?
- Use the 30-percent question as a self-attack: if a cheaper you is possible, someone will build it.
- Think of honesty as a Cow when everyone else in the category is shouting more.

## Anti-patterns
- **Upside-down ads, green bow ties, shock for its own sake**: sneezers' conversations about you will not be positive.
- **Switching ad agencies again** (Burger King) or laying off ten thousand (Motorola) instead of changing the product.
- **Entering an incremental price war** without a structural cost advantage.

## Worked Example
Godin's Hallmark brainstorm, reconstructed. Hallmark.com grew on an ideavirus (recipients of e-cards became senders) and makes money on gift certificates. Idea: when a Gold Crown Club member is about to send an e-card, ask if he wants to see how many points he would get for also sending a gift certificate. Show a random number between 100 points and one million. Most win little; occasionally someone is eligible for a huge number. The focused, listening group will risk a click to find out. That shifts the conversation from "send an e-card" to "talk to Hallmark about gift certificates," and the motivated sneezers send them. It only becomes a home run if recipients start sending certificates too. "Isn't a million points a lot to give away? Exactly. It's Purple."

## Key Takeaways
1. Outrageous only counts when it has a purpose and lives in the product.
2. Try telling the truth where the category exaggerates.
3. Answer the factory objection with a blank-sheet session with engineers and customers, not another campaign.
4. Do not compete on cheap unless you can leap the pricing by changing how the thing is made.
5. Combine permission, a group with a known problem, and sneezers, then make the offer big enough to be Purple.
