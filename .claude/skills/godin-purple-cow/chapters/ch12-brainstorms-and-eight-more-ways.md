# Chapter 12: Brainstorms, and Eight More Ways to Bring the Cow to Work (book pages 122–137)

Covers the sections: Brainstorms (thirty-four examples) · Salt Is Not Boring: Eight More Ways to Bring the Cow to Work

## Core Idea
"There aren't too many unexplored areas of innovation, just unexplored combinations." This is the book's checklist: thirty-four examples of an attribute pushed to its limit, plus eight prompts for a product as boring as salt. Ask of your latest offering: is it the mostest of any of these?

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Explore the limits**: "What if you're the cheapest, the fastest, the slowest, the hottest, the coldest, the easiest, the most efficient, the loudest, the most hated, the copycat, the outsider, the hardest, the oldest, the newest, the... most! If there's a limit, you should (must) test it."
- **Go where the competition is not**: Wal-Mart's banner while chasing Amazon: "You can't out-Amazon Amazon." Democrats cannot out-Republican Republicans; Reebok cannot out-Nike Nike; JetBlue did not out-American American. "Once someone stakes out a limit, you're foolish to attempt a pale imitation... The farther the better."
- **Distinct or extinct**: three music-business companies (Oasis CD Duplication, Airshow Mastering, CDBaby) thrive in a dying industry by obsessing over independent musicians and treating partners with respect. "Targeting a thriving niche in a slow-moving industry can work, if you're prepared to invest what it takes to be remarkable."
- **Eight more ways to bring the Cow to work** (Salt Is Not Boring), the exact prompts:
  1. "Is your product more boring than salt? Unlikely. So come up with a list of ten ways to change the product (not the hype) to make it appeal to a sliver of your audience."
  2. "Think small... Think of the smallest conceivable market, and describe a product that overwhelms it with its remarkability. Go from there."
  3. "Outsource. If the factory is giving you a hard time about jazzing up the product, go elsewhere... After it works, the factory will probably be happy to take the product back."
  4. "Build and use a permission asset. Once you have the ability to talk directly to your most loyal customers, it gets much easier to develop and sell amazing things."
  5. "Copy. Not from your industry, but from any other industry. Find an industry more dull than yours, discover who's remarkable (it won't take long), and do what they did."
  6. "Go one more. Or two more. Identify a competitor who's generally regarded as at the edges, and outdo them... Even better, and even safer, do the opposite of what they're doing."
  7. "Find things that are 'just not done' in your industry, and do them." JetBlue nearly set a passenger dress code; a plastic surgeon could offer gift certificates; a publisher could put a book on sale; Stew Leonard's let customers pick their own strawberries and sales doubled.
  8. "Ask, 'Why not?' Almost everything you don't do has no good reason for it. Almost everything you don't do is the result of fear or inertia or a historical lack of someone asking, 'Why not?'"

## Key Concepts: the thirty-four attributes, grouped
- **Durability**: Craftsman tools, guaranteed forever, recommended by everyone who uses tools. KitchenAid lost sneezing bakers by cheapening its mixers. Remarkable durability, or its lack, is a story either way.
- **Shown off by users**: OXO gadgets sell to non-cooks who saw them in cooks' kitchens.
- **Simplicity**: Yahoo! beat AltaVista with a simple fast interface, then Google beat Yahoo! the same way (two buttons). The anonymous emailer who sends Google only the word count of its home page. Yahoo!'s is over five hundred.
- **Serve the unserved moment**: Enterprise picks you up when your car is in the shop; not at the airport, not for business travelers.
- **Hard to learn, so hard to leave**: the Bloomberg terminal, over $1,000 a month and tricky, kept because the expertise is sunk. Linux's flaws created a devoted core that improved it.
- **Personalized**: Four Seasons brings Ted Leonsis his iced tea, tall thin glass, carafe of sugar water, unasked. His guests notice.
- **Guarantee as story**: L.L. Bean will refund burned pants if you send the ashes.
- **Very expensive and custom**: Jesse James's $100,000 handmade motorcycles with a multi-year wait; watches at $50,000 with two-year waiting lists because they are complicated.
- **Annoying on purpose**: the Hummer, too big and too ugly, loved by buyers who enjoy annoying the rest of us.
- **Making news**: executives fly to Cannes because something remarkable will happen. "There's a big difference between making the news and making news."
- **One thing only**: growafrog.com (pet tadpoles for twenty years), Just Bulbs, Just Shades. Obsessive focus makes you the obvious choice.
- **Ugly as conversation starter**: Volvo.
- **A cause that is easy to spread**: the CD long box campaign; small concession, easy news, big companies wanted to look green.
- **Bad taste that finds its audience**: John Waters's unwatchable early films made his reputation with the bizarre-loving early adopters; Hairspray is on Broadway.
- **Cheap remarkable service**: a teenager in a clown suit doing magic every weekend filled a restaurant with families.
- **Changing the store**: Best Buy removed commissions; $250 million to $23 billion.
- **A message behind the offer**: the three-pound $50 steak refunded if you finish it; hard ski slopes; unlimited DVD rentals.
- **Certainty over speed**: L.L. Bean moved to FedEx for the delivery date and real-time tracking, not speed.
- **Best at something worth measuring**: the fastest motorcycle, 0 to 250 in 14 seconds, ten buyers at $250,000 who will never go that fast.
- **Fixing it fast**: Apple sent an Airborne truck within two hours and returned the PowerBook in 48. Profitable via AppleCare, plus the story.
- **Loudest and quietest**: the loudest car stereo (eight times a 747) and premiums for the last decibel of quiet (Bose on a plane, watch the sneeze happen).
- **Form plus function at the price**: IKEA, over 25 percent of furniture in its price range in Europe and the US, without advertising.
- **The remarkable jackpot**: lottery sales rise exponentially at record jackpots though the odds are worse.
- **Danger**: unfiltered cigarettes, high-proof alcohol, kiteboarding.
- **Risk avoidance**: a lawyer who only defends one kind of lawsuit; the client in trouble seeks the lowest downside.
- **Beautiful staff, distinctive uniforms, a memorable voice**: Sotheby's, pink-striped ice cream stands, James Earl Jones on Directory Assistance, Moviefone's "annoying voice."
- **A doctor who spends time**: a dozen referrals from one patient.
- **Exceptionally nice people** as a Cow, unless you are a real monopoly (the Postal Service, the DMV).
- **Honor system**: Frontière's open bottle of wine on every table versus Brock's "SORRY, NO SHARING SALAD BAR." Two glasses pay for the bottle at wholesale.
- **Stupid rules and mean waiters**: Godin's third law of restaurant dining, friendliness inversely proportional to quality. Johnny's Pizza, Louis's no-ketchup, the soup line. "Could you improve the fame of your retail establishment by creating stupid rules and hiring truly mean waiters? Sure, probably."
- **Salt**: Morton made it a commodity for fifty years; French sea salt gets $20 a pound; Diamond Kosher sales up millions because it tastes better on food.

## Mental Models
- Run your offering down the limits list. Which attribute could you push to "most"?
- Look outside the industry for the dullest neighbor with one remarkable player.
- Treat every "that's just not done" as a candidate, and every "we don't do that" as a missing "why not?"

## Anti-patterns
- **Pale imitation of a staked-out limit**: the farther from the leader, the better.
- **Making the news instead of making news**: publicity hype without something to say.
- **Assuming a commodity cannot have a Cow**: salt says otherwise.

## Worked Example
Apply the eight prompts to a consulting practice. (1) Ten product changes, not hype: publish the diagnostic, guarantee a decision in thirty days, refuse projects without a named owner. (2) Smallest market: mid-size firms with one data team and a new CEO. (3) Outsource what the "factory" resists: hire a designer for the deliverable. (4) Permission asset: a list of past clients who asked to hear about the next thing. (5) Copy a duller industry: what does the remarkable accounting firm do? (6) Go one more than the edgiest competitor, or do the opposite. (7) Not done: publish your prices; put a proposal on sale. (8) For each thing you do not do, ask why not, and write the answer down.

## Key Takeaways
1. Innovation is unexplored combinations. Test every limit.
2. Never try to out-leader the leader. Go where they are not, far away.
3. Change the product, not the hype. Ten ways, aimed at a sliver.
4. Think of the smallest market you could overwhelm.
5. Copy the remarkable player in a duller industry.
6. Do what is "just not done." Ask "why not?" of everything you do not do.
