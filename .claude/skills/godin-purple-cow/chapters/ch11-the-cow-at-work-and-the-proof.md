# Chapter 11: The Cow at Work, the Life Cycle, and the Proof (book pages 110–121)

Covers the sections: When the Cow Looks for a Job · Case Study: Tracey the Publicist · Case Study: Robyn Waters Gets It · Case Study: So Popular, No One Goes There Anymore · Is It About Passion? · True Facts

## Core Idea
The same rules run a career: a résumé is advertising, references are the Cow, and the time to be remarkable is when you are not looking. The Cow is a phase of the life cycle, not a permanent state. And you do not need passion to adopt it, just the realization that nothing else works, which the top-100 brand list proves.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The résumé is advertising**: sending it to hundreds of employers is interruption of people with no interest in you, and it produces no word of mouth. "Remarkable people often don't even have a résumé. Instead, they rely on sneezers who are quick to recommend them." They do outrageous jobs on high-profile projects, take risks, fail big, and get better projects next time. "If you're thinking about being a Purple Cow, the time to do it is when you're not looking for a job." Takeaways: "Your references are your résumé. A standard résumé is nothing but an opportunity for a prospective employer to turn you down. A sheaf of over-the-top references, on the other hand, begs for a meeting." And: millions of résumés sit in a pile on Monster; do today what makes sure you are never in that pile.
- **The narrowest possible niche** (Tracey): hundreds of form letters to marketing directors failed; anyone who needs a PR firm has one. So she became the world's best publicist to plastic surgeons. Journals, conferences, doctors, lists. "Everyone else has this audience as part of their portfolio. For Tracey, they are her portfolio." If your job depended on hiring the best person for that exact audience, who would you hire?
- **The Cow is part of the life cycle**: "You can't live it all the time (too risky, too expensive, too tiring), but when you need to grow or need to introduce something new, it's your best shot."
- **You don't need passion**: "You don't need passion to create a Purple Cow. Nor do you need an awful lot of creativity. What you need is the insight to realize that you have no other choice." Ten products at $10 million each beats $100 million of TV on one; if all ten fail you learned ten ways that do not work. "Things that have to work rarely do anymore."
- **The proof** (True Facts, Interbrand's top 100 brands of 2002): seventy were valuable more than twenty-five years ago, built with heavy advertising when it was easy and cheap. Of the remaining thirty, half were built almost entirely through word of mouth (HP, Oracle, Nintendo, SAP, Canon, IKEA, Sun, Yahoo!, Ericsson, Motorola, Amazon, Prada, Starbucks, Ralph Lauren, Armani). Some got value through market power (Cisco, Microsoft). Apple used both remarkable ads and word of mouth. Only a handful (Compaq, Dell, Nike, Gap, MTV, AOL, maybe Nivea) built the old way, and only AOL did it in the last ten years of media excess. "If only 6 percent of the most valuable brands used the now-obsolete strategy... why do you believe this strategy will work for you?" Takeaway: "Do you want to grow? If you do, you need to embrace the Cow."

## Key Concepts
- **Robyn Waters, Target**: VP of "trend, design, and technical specifications." Persuaded Michael Graves to make a teapot for Target; finds amazingly cheap but cool flatware. Exclusive items that would be cool at any price and are amazing when cheap. "Cool products that appeal to people who both buy new stuff and talk about it a lot are the core of Target's strategy." No big ad budget needed to beat Sears and Kmart.
- **Stew Leonard's**: petting zoo, service policy carved in a 6,000-pound granite block, unique products, robotic mooing cows, a violin-playing chicken. Grew tenfold, made Ripley's. The son's store: too popular and boring. Nothing organic, nothing unusual, nothing remarkably cheap, service merely okay, a suggestion box nobody answers. He "exchanged me (someone with a food and service otaku and a big-time sneezer) for ten ordinary grocery consumers." Brilliant short-run milking; it will not open a store in Houston.
- **The skeptics**: people who say "Wal-Mart won't take it" or "we can't afford product failures" are not moved by passion. They want what works. That is why the argument is evidence, not inspiration.
- **South Park's focus group**: 1.5 out of 10 with women, three of them cried. The group that mattered, adolescent boys, spread it into a monster hit. "It's not about being weird. It's about being irresistible to a tiny group of easily reached sneezers with otaku."

## Mental Models
- Treat the career like a product line: the remarkable project you do now is the launch; the references are the word of mouth.
- Read a market as a portfolio-versus-portfolio question: whose whole portfolio is this audience?
- When the parking lot is full, ask whether you are milking or building, and whether the milk will travel to a town where no one knows you.

## Anti-patterns
- **Networking your résumé** into a pile.
- **Focus groups as a green light**: "If the focus group likes it, they're probably wrong."
- **One product that must work this Christmas**: start working on your résumé.
- **Dumbing down for the masses** and calling it growth. It is wealth extraction from a brand someone else built.

## Worked Example
Tracey's repositioning, step by step. Old approach: general PR shop, hundreds of FedEx packages to marketing directors across the Northeast, expensive, no response. Diagnosis: those who need PR already have it, and a cold package cannot displace an incumbent. Move: narrow from PR, to pharmaceuticals (her background), to publicists for plastic surgeons. Build: know every journal, conference, and most of the doctors; hold the lists and contacts. Result: for a pharma company that must reach plastic surgeons, she is "the one and only exceptional choice." The niche is small enough to dominate and valuable enough to pay.

## Key Takeaways
1. Be remarkable when you are not looking. Let references and sneezers do the searching.
2. Pick the narrowest niche where you can be the only exceptional choice.
3. The Cow is for growth and launches. Milking is fine, but know which phase you are in.
4. Launch ten small bets, not one big one that has to work.
5. Argue from the brand list, not from passion, when you face skeptics.
