# Chapter 7: The Opposite of Remarkable Is Very Good (book pages 67–78)

Covers the sections: The Opposite of "Remarkable" · The Pearl in the Bottle · The Parody Paradox · Seventy-two Pearl Jam Albums · Case Study: Curad · Sit There, Don't Just Do Something · Case Study: United States Postal Service

## Core Idea
"The opposite of 'remarkable' is 'very good.'" Quality is not the question. What gets talked about is the unexpected: the packaging, the parody-worthy voice, the collectible, the honest gift to your core fans. And when you have nothing remarkable to add, doing nothing beats doing something mediocre.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Very good is invisible**: an airline that gets you there safely is doing what is supposed to happen; nobody tells anyone. Remarkable is "horrible beyond belief" or "so unexpected... that you need to share it." Factories set quality requirements and meet them. "That's boring." Takeaway: "Are you making very good stuff? How fast can you stop?"
- **The packaging is the product** (Dr. Bronner's): no advertising, growing share, discovered in a friend's guest bathroom by reading the thousands of words on the bottle. "The packaging is very much part of the experience of using the product." Takeaway: "Buy a bottle of Dr. Bronner's. Now, working with your factory and your designers, Bronnify a variation of one of your products."
- **The parody paradox**: "If you can show up in a parody, it means you've got something unique, something worth poking fun at." J. Peterman's catalog was so over the top it became a Seinfeld character. L.L. Bean or Lands' End could never be parodied; they are safe and boring. "The same word of mouth that can make your product a huge hit can also lead to someone's snickering at you." Takeaway: "How could you modify your product or service so that you'd show up on the next episode of Saturday Night Live?"
- **Sell to the converted** (Pearl Jam): seventy-two live albums in two years, sold on their own site to fans who gave permission. All profitable. Big fans bring new fans. Takeaway: "Do you have the email addresses of the 20 percent of your customer base that loves what you do? If not, start getting them. If you do, what could you make for these customers that would be super-special?"
- **Sit there, don't just do something**: marketing to justify a department's existence is a compromise (budget compromise: new slogan instead of new product; product compromise: something less radical). "Almost without exception, these compromises are worse than doing nothing." Ben & Jerry's did nothing unless it had a super-cool flavor. McIntosh launches a few amplifiers a decade. "Marketing just to keep busy is worse than nothing at all."

## Key Concepts
- **Prell versus Dr. Bronner's**: Prell's pearl-in-the-bottle worked only while TV commercials worked. Bronner's has an "unfair advantage against anything the big guys can develop" in a world without effective ads.
- **Committee sanding**: each well-meaning participant speaks for a constituency that might not like it; the result is boring and safe.
- **Ninety-seven percent of records lose money**: the interrupt-strangers model is broken. Breakthrough acts are now blind luck plus a small group of sneezers.
- **Collectible**: Curad printed characters on bandages. Kids loved them, parents too, and the first kid at school made every other kid want them. Curad took share from Band-Aid. Takeaway: "Could you make a collectible version of your product?"
- **ZIP+4**: the Postal Service, with the most timid audience imaginable, diffused a change across billions of records. Two reasons: a game-changing benefit (faster delivery, lower cost for bulk mailers, felt immediately on the bottom line) and a deliberate few early adopters who were technical, price-sensitive, and positioned to sneeze to less astute mailers.

## Mental Models
- Use the "would anyone tell a friend?" test instead of the quality bar. Very good does not clear it.
- Think of the tin: Altoids, J. Peterman, Bronner's. The container is a script the sneezer can hand over.
- The lesson of ZIP+4: "The more intransigent your market, the more crowded the marketplace, the busier your customers, the more you need the Purple Cow. Half-measures will fail."

## Anti-patterns
- **Confusing very good with virus-worthy**: "this is not a discussion about quality at all."
- **Refreshing the line with mediocre messaging** so your remaining fans have nothing to spread.
- **Steering away from anything that could be laughed at**: that is steering away from being interesting.

## Worked Example
Pearl Jam's system, reconstructed. Break through once (hard work, some luck, hits, headliner status). Then, instead of trying to repeat the Top 40 game, rally the core. Record every show. Put all seventy-two on the website. Sell to people who have already said yes, so the cost of each sale is tiny. Delight them so much they indoctrinate friends. Keep the base satisfied with remarkable products so there is very little leakage. The band turned a profit on every album. The takeaway is the permission asset: the list of the 20 percent who love you.

## Key Takeaways
1. Stop making very good things. Make things people must share.
2. Make the packaging, container, or voice part of the product.
3. Aim to be parodied. It is the sign of a Cow.
4. Get the addresses of the 20 percent who love you and make them something special.
5. With nothing remarkable to add, do nothing. Take a season off and reintroduce the classics.
6. In a timid market, only a dramatic improvement in what the right customers care about will move.
