# Analytical Lenses

Use this file to generate candidate insights. Do not use it as a mandatory output outline.

## Contents

- Lens selection rule
- Fast selection map
- Lens catalog
- Method visibility

## Lens selection rule

Choose the smallest useful set of lenses based on the structure of the problem, usually two to four. One lens is enough when it exposes the material mechanism. A lens earns its place only if it reveals a contradiction, dependency, implication, or opportunity.

Do not apply all lenses. More lenses usually create more words, not more insight.

## Fast selection map

| Signal in the material | Start with |
|---|---|
| A claim depends on assumptions no one has tested | Inversion, first principles |
| The same problem appears repeatedly | Pattern recognition, structural analysis |
| Several actors or processes interact | Systems thinking, power and incentives |
| A decision creates delayed consequences | Second and third-order thinking, probabilistic thinking |
| The framing feels stale or binary | Lateral thinking, perspective shift |
| The explanation has too many moving parts | Occam's razor, first principles |
| Important information is absent | Negative-space analysis, inversion |
| An analogy may unlock a new mechanism | Cross-disciplinary parallels, then evidence testing |

## Lens catalog

### 1. Inversion thinking

**Use when:** The obvious strategy or explanation is accepted without testing.

**Ask:**

- What would make the desired outcome impossible?
- What if the central assumption were false?
- What behavior would we expect if the opposite explanation were true?

**Trap:** Treating the opposite as automatically correct. Inversion generates hypotheses; evidence selects among them.

### 2. First principles

**Use when:** The problem is defined through inherited language, conventions, or bundled assumptions.

**Ask:**

- What must be true regardless of current process or technology?
- What is the irreducible job, constraint, or outcome?
- Which parts are facts and which are conventions?

**Trap:** Reducing away stakeholder, institutional, or behavioral realities that remain causal.

### 3. Pattern recognition

**Use when:** Multiple examples, time periods, teams, or signals are available.

**Ask:**

- What repeats despite changes in context?
- Where does the sequence recur?
- What exceptions reveal the boundary of the pattern?

**Trap:** Calling coincidence a pattern without enough observations or a plausible mechanism.

### 4. Deep structural analysis

**Use when:** Surface events vary but outcomes remain similar.

**Ask:**

- What rules, architectures, incentives, definitions, or dependencies keep reproducing the result?
- Which element is upstream of several visible symptoms?
- What remains unchanged when people, tools, or tactics change?

**Trap:** Preferring a complicated hidden structure when a simpler explanation fits better.

### 5. Systems thinking

**Use when:** The outcome emerges from interacting components rather than one cause.

**Ask:**

- What are the reinforcing and balancing feedback loops?
- Where are delays, bottlenecks, and handoffs?
- Which local optimization makes the whole system worse?
- Where could an intervention move the problem elsewhere?

**Trap:** Producing a comprehensive system map without identifying a decision-relevant leverage point.

### 6. Second and third-order thinking

**Use when:** A proposed action has incentives, adaptations, or delayed effects.

**Ask:**

- What happens next if this works?
- How will other actors adapt?
- What new dependency, expectation, risk, or option is created?
- What becomes harder to reverse?

**Trap:** Inventing long causal chains with no evidence for the intermediate steps.

### 7. Lateral thinking

**Use when:** The current categories or solution space constrain the answer.

**Ask:**

- What if this is the wrong problem category?
- What would a radically different actor optimize for?
- Which constraint could be removed, exchanged, or made useful?

**Trap:** Rewarding novelty that has no relationship to the evidence or decision.

### 8. Probabilistic thinking

**Use when:** Evidence is incomplete, outcomes are uncertain, or several explanations remain plausible.

**Ask:**

- What are the competing hypotheses?
- Which evidence raises or lowers each hypothesis?
- What is the base rate, if known?
- Which new observation would most change confidence?

**Trap:** Using precise percentages when the evidence supports only qualitative confidence.

### 9. Occam's razor

**Use when:** The explanation depends on many assumptions or stakeholders are overwhelmed by complexity.

**Ask:**

- What is the smallest explanation that accounts for the evidence?
- Which assumptions can be removed without reducing explanatory power?
- Is one upstream mechanism producing several symptoms?

**Trap:** Choosing the simplest story even when it omits a material contradiction.

### 10. Perspective shifts and counterfactuals

**Use when:** One stakeholder's framing dominates or causality is unclear.

**Ask:**

- How would another stakeholder describe the same situation?
- What would we observe if the claimed cause were absent?
- What would have happened without the intervention?
- Which conclusion changes when the unit of analysis changes?

**Trap:** Treating imagined counterfactuals as observed evidence.

### 11. Power and incentive analysis

**Use when:** Formal roles, decision rights, metrics, or political risk shape behavior.

**Ask:**

- Who can decide, block, delay, define success, or control information?
- Who benefits from ambiguity or the status quo?
- What behavior do the incentives reward, regardless of stated intent?
- Who bears the downside while someone else captures the upside?

**Trap:** Assuming bad intent when ordinary incentives or constraints explain behavior.

### 12. Cross-disciplinary parallels

**Use when:** A mechanism in another field may illuminate the current problem.

**Ask:**

- Where else does a similar structure appear?
- What transfers at the mechanism level, not merely the metaphor level?
- Which important differences make the analogy fail?

**Trap:** Using resemblance as proof. Analogies generate candidates; source evidence must support the final claim.

### 13. Negative-space analysis

**Use when:** Omissions, silence, unavailable measures, or absent actors may be informative.

**Ask:**

- What would normally be present if the obvious explanation were complete?
- Which outcome, stakeholder, comparison, or failure mode is not discussed?
- Is the absence caused by irrelevance, measurement limits, incentives, or avoidance?

**Trap:** Treating missing information as evidence of a preferred explanation rather than as an uncertainty.

## Method visibility

Do not add an "Analytical Methods Applied" section by default.

If the user asks to see the methods, report only:

- The lenses that produced surviving insights.
- What each lens revealed.
- Where the lens did not produce sufficient evidence.

Do not narrate private chain-of-thought or every discarded candidate.
