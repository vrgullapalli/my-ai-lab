# Evidence and Confidence

Use this file whenever the analysis involves citations, uncertainty, external facts, current information, or ambiguous source boundaries.

## Contents

- Choose the evidence mode
- Maintain an evidence ledger
- Classify evidence by diagnostic value
- Use evidence proportionately
- Cite precisely
- Calibrate confidence
- State what would change the conclusion
- Handle stakeholder and motive claims carefully
- Bias and privacy checks

## 1. Choose the evidence mode

### Source-bound mode

This is the default.

- Use only the material supplied by the user.
- Do not introduce outside facts as established evidence.
- General knowledge may help generate questions, but it must not silently become support for a conclusion.
- State when the source cannot support the requested conclusion.

### Verification-enabled mode

Use when the user requests research or fact-checking, or when current or high-stakes facts materially affect the conclusion and tools are available.

- Separate supplied-source evidence from external evidence.
- Prefer authoritative, primary, and current sources.
- Cite external factual claims using the host environment's required citation format.
- Record the date or time sensitivity when it matters.
- Do not say "verified" unless verification was actually performed.

If the required tools are unavailable, state the limitation and keep the conclusion appropriately narrow.

## 2. Maintain an evidence ledger

A private ledger prevents evidence and interpretation from blending.

| Label | Meaning | Example form |
|---|---|---|
| Observed | Explicit in the source | "The metric definition changed in Q3." |
| Inferred | Derived from connected observations | "The apparent improvement may partly reflect measurement change." |
| Hypothesized | Plausible but insufficiently supported | "Teams may be optimizing for approval rather than adoption." |
| Unknown | Missing information that affects confidence | "No pre-change baseline is provided." |
| Recommended | Proposed response | "Recalculate the trend using a stable definition." |

Do not expose ledger IDs unless they improve traceability in a deep analysis. The category distinctions must remain visible in the final response.

## 3. Classify evidence by diagnostic value

For each serious candidate explanation, distinguish:

- **Supporting evidence:** More likely if the candidate is true.
- **Disconfirming evidence:** Less likely if the candidate is true or directly inconsistent with it.
- **Non-diagnostic evidence:** Compatible with several explanations and therefore weak for choosing among them.
- **Missing evidence:** An absent observation that would materially separate competing explanations.

Do not count every source fact as support. Evidence is useful when it helps discriminate between explanations.

## 4. Use evidence proportionately

Evidence strength generally decreases in this order:

1. Direct, specific statements or measurements in the supplied material.
2. Consistent patterns across multiple independent observations.
3. Structural inferences supported by several related observations.
4. Single-observation inferences.
5. Analogies and general domain expectations.
6. Intuition or speculation.

Items 5 and 6 can generate hypotheses. They cannot independently establish an insight.

## 5. Cite precisely

When source locations are available:

- Cite the smallest section, line range, page, table, or quotation needed.
- Paraphrase where possible. Do not fabricate quotations.
- Link each insight to the evidence that supports that exact claim.
- Do not attach one citation to a paragraph containing several unsupported conclusions.

When formal citations are unavailable, identify the evidence precisely enough that a reader can locate it, such as "the section describing the Q3 metric change."

## 6. Calibrate confidence

### High confidence

Use when:

- Several specific observations support the same mechanism, or one observation is decisive.
- Competing explanations fit materially worse.
- No major contradiction remains unresolved.

### Medium confidence

Use when:

- The inference is coherent and evidence-grounded.
- Evidence is limited, indirect, or partly contradictory.
- At least one plausible alternative remains.

### Low confidence

Use when:

- The claim is mainly a hypothesis.
- Critical evidence is absent.
- Several explanations fit similarly well.

Do not convert qualitative confidence into a numerical probability unless the source supports calibration.

## 7. State what would change the conclusion

For each substantive insight, identify the smallest piece of evidence that would most update confidence:

- A stable comparison or baseline.
- A counterexample.
- Another stakeholder's account.
- A measure of downstream behavior.
- Decision-rights or incentive information.
- Evidence from before and after an intervention.

This is more useful than a generic call for "more data."

## 8. Handle stakeholder and motive claims carefully

- Infer a stakeholder only when role, decision, or consequence is evident.
- Label the inference when the source does not name the stakeholder.
- Do not infer intent when incentives, constraints, or process can explain behavior.
- Distinguish formal authority from actual influence.
- Treat silence or omission as an unknown first, not proof of avoidance.

## 9. Bias and privacy checks

- Consider whether the source privileges one stakeholder's perspective.
- Avoid demographic or personal inferences that are unnecessary to the analysis.
- Do not reproduce sensitive details unless they are required for the user's task.
- Note when the available evidence was selected, measured, or framed by an interested party.
