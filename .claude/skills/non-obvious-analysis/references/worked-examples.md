# Worked Examples

These examples demonstrate the quality standard. They are synthetic and must not be treated as evidence for another task.

## Contents

- Example 1: Engagement improved, but the decision did not

## Example 1: Engagement improved, but the decision did not

### Supplied material

A brand team reports:

- Email engagement increased from 18 percent to 31 percent.
- The vendor changed the definition of an "engaged recipient" during the same quarter.
- Prescribing behavior did not materially change.
- Field teams reported receiving more basic clarification questions after the campaign.
- The program was declared successful based on the engagement increase.

The brand lead must decide whether to scale the campaign.

### Weak analysis

> Engagement increased, but prescribing did not. The team should look at more metrics and align stakeholders.

Why it fails:

- It mostly restates the source.
- "Look at more metrics" is generic.
- It does not identify a mechanism or a changed decision.

### Strong analysis

## What this appears to be

- **Surface reading:** The campaign improved engagement but has not yet produced downstream behavior change.
- **Primary stakeholder:** The brand lead deciding whether to scale.
- **Stakeholder tension:** A visible success metric supports expansion, while downstream evidence does not.
- **Core problem:** The team may be deciding whether to scale the campaign before establishing whether the measured improvement represents persuasion, measurement change, or confusion.

## What may actually be going on

### 1. The clearest success signal may come from the measurement system, not the campaign

- **Why it is easy to miss:** The engagement trend is visually compelling and is presented as a before-and-after performance result.
- **Evidence:** The definition of an engaged recipient changed in the same quarter that engagement increased, while downstream behavior remained stable.
- **Counterevidence or competing explanation:** The campaign may still have produced a real lift; the source does not show the engagement trend recalculated under a stable definition.
- **Interpretation:** Inferred.
- **Why it matters:** Scaling on the reported lift could allocate budget to an effect that is partly definitional rather than behavioral.
- **Confidence:** Medium, because the timing and outcome divergence support the inference, but no recalculation under a stable definition is provided.
- **What would change this conclusion:** Recalculating both periods using the same engagement definition.

### 2. More engagement may indicate unresolved meaning rather than stronger conviction

- **Why it is easy to miss:** Interaction is usually treated as progress, while clarification questions are treated as a separate field issue.
- **Evidence:** Engagement and basic questions increased, but prescribing behavior did not materially change.
- **Counterevidence or competing explanation:** The questions may reflect healthy interest, field execution differences, or unrelated timing effects rather than message confusion.
- **Interpretation:** Hypothesized.
- **Why it matters:** The next intervention may need to repair message clarity rather than increase reach or frequency.
- **Confidence:** Medium-low, because the questions could have other causes and their content is not provided.
- **What would change this conclusion:** Coding the clarification questions by topic and comparing exposed and unexposed accounts.

## The insight that matters most

The measurement-definition change matters most because it affects whether the scale decision is based on a comparable outcome at all. It should be resolved before interpreting the campaign's downstream effect.

## What changes now

- **Reconsider:** Treat the reported engagement lift as provisional rather than as evidence sufficient for scale.
- **Next action or test:** Recalculate engagement using a stable definition, then examine whether question types indicate confusion or decision progression.
- **Critical uncertainty:** How much of the lift remains after the metric is normalized.

### Why this passes

- Both insights rely on specific evidence.
- The second insight is more tentative and is labeled accordingly.
- Each insight changes a different decision.
- The analysis returns two insights rather than forcing four.

## Example 2: The source cannot support four surprising insights

### Supplied material

- The project launched on schedule.
- All invited stakeholders attended the launch meeting.
- A dashboard is available.

The user asks for four "shocking" insights about the project's success.

### Correct response shape

## What the material supports

The material supports only that the project launched on schedule, attendance was complete, and a dashboard exists. These are delivery and participation facts, not evidence of adoption, quality, outcome, or durable value.

## What it does not yet support

It does not support four non-obvious conclusions about success. It also does not establish whether the dashboard is used, trusted, decision-relevant, or connected to an outcome.

## What would unlock deeper analysis

The smallest useful evidence would be:

- What decision or workflow the dashboard is meant to change.
- Actual usage after launch.
- A before-and-after outcome or credible comparison.
- Evidence of disagreement, workarounds, or stakeholder behavior after delivery.

### Why this passes

- It refuses the forced quantity without refusing the task.
- It distinguishes delivery evidence from outcome evidence.
- It identifies the minimum evidence needed for a deeper conclusion.
- It does not invent stakeholder motives or project results.
