# What WATSON actually is

A research assistant that takes **one topic** and hands back **five content ideas**. That's the whole contract. You give it a word. It gives you angles.

It does not write anything. Her line: *it surfaces the raw material and shows you where it connects to who you already are.*

## The one idea underneath it

Normal AI research goes **deeper into your topic**. WATSON goes **sideways, out of it** — then comes back and explains why the outside thing and your thing are the same shape.

That's the entire product. Everything in the spec exists to force that sideways move and then prove it wasn't a stretch.

---

## The four things that make it work

### 1. It reads your positioning every single time

Not once at setup. Every run, in full — who you are, what you argue, who you're talking to. So every idea comes back already filtered through you, not through "what's trending."

### 2. It searches wide *before* it judges anything

First it grabs 20+ sources and dumps them into a 25-row table. **No filtering at this stage.** It's not allowed to start liking or disliking things yet.

That sounds like a small detail. It's the whole trick — the moment you start filtering early, you filter toward what you already think, and you're back to reading the same ten articles as everyone else.

### 3. Every idea must come with a written argument

This is her "Rule 7 — the connection paragraph is sacred."

For each idea, WATSON has to write 2–3 sentences explaining *why* the outside thing and your thing share a **structure** — same mechanism, same failure, same constraint. Not "they're both about AI."

**If it can't write that paragraph convincingly, the idea is deleted.** Not scored lower. Deleted.

This is clever because it turns a vague standard ("is this a good connection?") into something concrete: either a defensible paragraph exists or it doesn't. You can't fake it past a reader.

### 4. It asks you at most one question, ever

Before it starts, it decides: is this topic something searchable, or something personal to you?

- **Searchable** → it just goes. No questions.
- **Personal** → one question: *"want me to research related themes, or just work from your brand docs?"*

Then it acts. Never a second question. That's what keeps it from becoming an annoying chatbot.

She splits the agent into three kinds of file, and her framing is good:

- **Identity** — who it is. Three lines: its role, its mission, and one attitude ("despise generic takes and keyword-matching disguised as insight").

## What a "lens" is

A lens is a **kind of thing to go looking for** — not a topic, a *type of source*.

Give WATSON "Nano Banana 2" and without lenses it does what you'd do: search the product name, read the tech press, summarize. One kind of source, one kind of finding.

The lenses force it to ask the same topic thirteen different questions. *What's the news here? What are people angry about? Who thinks the consensus is wrong? What behavioral pattern explains this? What does this remind me of from somewhere else entirely?*

Each one drags back a different flavor of material.

---

## Why they're doing double duty

This is the part that's easy to miss. She describes the search strategy as: **2–3 broad queries, then targeted queries for each research category.**

So the lenses aren't a filing system applied *after* the search. They're **the search plan**. Each lens generates its own queries.

That matters because "sort your findings into categories" is cosmetic — you can only sort what you already found. "Run a query per category" changes what shows up in the first place. If the contrarian lens is on, something in the pipeline is actively hunting for people who disagree, rather than hoping a disagreement happens to appear in the results.

*(That reading is mine — she states the query strategy and the lens list separately, and this is the only way they fit together.)*

---

## The five that always run

These fire on every topic, no exceptions:

- **Breaking news** — what actually happened, the factual spine
- **Hot takes and opinions** — what people are saying about it
- **Contrarian voices** — who's arguing the opposite
- **Psychological / behavioral frameworks** — what known pattern of human behavior explains it
- **Analogies from other domains** — where else does this exact shape show up

**Why these five and not others:** the first two get you the ground truth. The last three are the ones that produce a non-obvious idea.

- *Contrarian* gives you tension, and tension is what an argument is made of.
- *Psychology* gives you the underlying mechanism — the reason a thing is happening, not just that it is.
- *Analogy* is the sideways jump. It's the one lens that's structurally guaranteed to bring back something from outside your niche.

Those three are always on because they're the ones that can't be skipped without the whole system collapsing back into summarizing. News and opinions you'd get anyway.

## The eight that switch on conditionally

These activate only when the topic warrants:

- Business angles
- Technical deep-dives
- Cultural / meme layer
- Historical parallels
- Data and stats
- Regulatory implications
- Creator stories
- Failure modes

**Why conditional:** running all thirteen on everything is expensive and mostly wasteful. A regulatory lens on "how Pixar runs feedback sessions" returns nothing. A cultural/meme lens on a dry B2B pricing change returns nothing.

Turning them off isn't laziness — it's keeping the research table from filling with rows that technically match and mean nothing. She's explicit that the table gets **no filtering** later, so junk that enters at this stage stays in and dilutes everything downstream.

## First: build-in-public breaks WATSON's premise

WATSON solves: *"I have a topic. I need an angle on it."* The topic is external, the angle is missing, so it searches outward to find one.

Build-in-public is the mirror image. You already **have** the material — a session, a decision, a thing that broke, a rule you deleted. Nothing is missing. What's missing is the answer to a much harder question:

> **Is this interesting to anyone but me?**

So the research isn't *discovery*. It's **verification and placement**. You're not hunting for an angle. You're finding out whether the thing you already did is novel, contested, already solved by someone else two years ago, or an instance of a pattern with a name.

That flip changes every lens.

---

## The input isn't a topic — it's an episode

WATSON takes a word. This takes a **dated thing that happened**, with a receipt:

- what you were trying to do
- what you actually did
- what broke
- what you decided
- what you'd do differently

If you can't fill those five, there's no post. That's a gate before research even starts — and it's the one that kills 80% of build-in-public content, which is mostly people narrating that they are building, which nobody wants.

---

## The five always-on lenses

### 1. Prior art — *"has someone already solved this?"*
Search hard for the thing you built. Not to be discouraged — to know where you stand.

Three outcomes, all useful:
- **Nobody has** → that's your headline, and now you can say it with evidence
- **Someone has, better** → the post becomes "I built the wrong thing, here's what I should have used," which is *more* interesting than the original
- **Someone has, differently** → the post becomes a comparison, and comparisons are the highest-value build-in-public genre there is

This lens is always on because it's the honesty gate. Publishing "I discovered X" when X has a Wikipedia page is the single fastest way to lose credibility.

### 2. Dissent — *"who thinks this approach is wrong?"*
Go find the people arguing against what you did. Not generic contrarians — people with a real position.

Two payoffs: you either strengthen your case by answering them, or you find out you're wrong before you publish. Both beat discovering it in the comments.

This is also where the tension in the post comes from. A build-in-public piece with no opposing view is a diary entry.

### 3. Mechanism — *"what is this an instance of?"*
Your specific thing broke in a specific way. What's the **named pattern** underneath it?

Feedback loops, entropy, Goodhart's law, principal-agent problems, second-system effect, the sunk cost you didn't notice. Behavioral economics, systems theory, organizational research.

This is the lens that makes a one-off story portable. "My governance rules stopped firing" is your problem. "Rules that measure nothing get ignored, and here's the research on why" is everyone's problem.

### 4. Cross-domain analogy — *"where else does this shape appear?"*
Straight from WATSON, and it survives the translation intact. The one lens guaranteed to bring back something from outside the AI-tooling conversation.

Given how you actually write, this is probably your strongest lens — the connection between an operating decision and something from a completely different field.

### 5. Audience stake — *"who has this problem on a Monday?"*
Is this pain live for other people right now, or is it an artifact of your particular setup?

The lens most people skip, and the reason most build-in-public content dies. If you can't find other people describing this problem in their own words — forums, threads, comments — you have a private hobby, not a post.

---

## The conditional ones

Switch on when the episode warrants:

- **Version reality** — did the tool change since you hit this? Build-in-public content rots faster than anything else; a post about a limitation that was fixed last month is embarrassing
- **Economics** — what did this cost, in money or time
- **The vendor's own claims** — what does the documentation say *should* happen versus what happened
- **Failure archive** — other people's postmortems on the same class of problem
- **Historical parallel** — the pre-computing version of this problem
- **Measurement** — is there data, or is this one anecdote wearing a suit
- **Regulatory / risk** — only for anything touching data, money, or someone else's information
- **Practitioner accounts** — people doing this work in the open, in their words

---

## The angles that actually work

Six shapes. Each needs a different lens mix.

| Angle | The move | Leans on |
|---|---|---|
| **The reversal** | "I was wrong about X, here's the evidence that changed it" | Dissent, prior art |
| **The kill** | "I deleted the thing I built. Here's why" | Mechanism, audience stake |
| **The constraint** | "I couldn't do X, so I did Y — and Y was better" | Analogy, prior art |
| **The boring fix** | "Everyone's building a complicated version of this. Mine is one file" | Prior art, economics |
| **The number** | "I assumed X. Measured it. It was 4× off" | Measurement, mechanism |
| **The open problem** | "Still broken. Here's where I'm stuck" | Dissent, audience stake |

**The reversal and the kill are the two that build the most trust,** and they're the two almost nobody publishes — because they require admitting you spent time on the wrong thing. That's exactly why they work.

**The open problem is the one most specific to building in public.** Everything else can be written retrospectively once it's resolved. Publishing an unresolved problem is what makes it *in public* rather than *after the fact* — and it's the only one that reliably gets useful replies.

---

## The gates I'd put on it

1. **Receipt gate** — a dated artifact exists. A commit, a decision entry, a file. No receipt, no post.
2. **Prior-art gate** — the search ran and the result is stated in the piece, even if it's "found nothing."
3. **Transferable gate** — one sentence naming what someone else does differently on Monday. Can't write it, kill it.
4. **Not-a-practitioner gate** — the story is the decision and the judgment, never the implementation. Fluent in it, not doing it.
5. **Unresolved-is-allowed gate** — an episode doesn't need a happy ending. Requiring one is what turns build-in-public into marketing.

## The one thing I'd change from WATSON

WATSON's fatal flaw is that it grades its own connection paragraphs. Here that gets worse, because the material is *yours* — you're emotionally invested in the thing being interesting.

So I'd add a step WATSON doesn't have: **research the strongest reason not to publish this**, and put it in the output next to the reason to publish. One paragraph each, side by side. You decide.

That's cheap, it's honest, and it's the only thing standing between build-in-public and a feed of people announcing that they are, in fact, building.
