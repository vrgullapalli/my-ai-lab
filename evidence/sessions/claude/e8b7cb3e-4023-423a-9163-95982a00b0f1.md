Here's what the research found, then what it means for the draft. You're right that it's too long — the standards say so too.

## What the docs say about context files

- **Anthropic (Claude Code memory docs):** "Specific, concise, well-structured instructions work best." Target under 200 lines for anything loaded every session. Use headers and bullets. One home per fact, no two files contradicting. Keep detail in topic files read on demand, reached by paths — "progressive disclosure."
- **Anthropic (context-engineering guide):** aim for "the minimal set of information that fully outlines your expected behavior." Keep "lightweight identifiers (file paths…)" and pull detail in at runtime.
- **OpenAI (AGENTS.md, prompt guidance):** plain Markdown, any headings. "State each instruction once." Nearest file wins. Default combined cap is 32 KiB — my draft alone is 38 KB.
- **Popular practice (AGENTS.md guides):** models follow roughly 150–200 instructions reliably; files grow by "reactive rule-adding" into "an unmaintainable mess." Point to other files; don't inline.

## What a positioning document should hold

- **April Dunford (the standard most PMMs use):** five parts, in this order — competitive alternatives, unique attributes, value, best-fit customer, market category. Positioning is "not equivalent to messaging," "not a tagline," "not your brand story," "not your vision." A fill-in-the-blank statement is "not only pointless but potentially dangerous."
- **Geoffrey Moore:** one sentence that forces choices — for [who], who [need], [name] is a [category] that [benefit]; unlike [alternative], it [differentiator]. Brief, memorable.
- **Popular AI example** (Brand-building-skills repo, `brand-context.md`): positioning is three fields — differentiation, competitors, market position — inside a one-page brand file every other skill reads first.
- **Not in any positioning standard:** proof narrative, process history, update rules, a provenance table. Those are records, not positioning.

> popular positioning frameworks converge on a clear principle:
> 
> > A positioning document records the current strategic position. It is not the research archive, biography, messaging library, or evidence dossier.
> 
> April Dunford defines positioning through five connected decisions: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, customer value, best-fit customers, and market category. She explicitly separates positioning from messaging, storytelling, taglines, vision, and branding. Those come afterward. [April Dunford’s positioning framework](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning)
> 
> ## What it should contain
> 
> For Venkat’s positioning, I recommend this structure:
> 
> | Section | What it must answer |
> |---|---|
> | Status and provenance | Is this draft or approved? Who owns it? When was it reviewed? Where is the evidence dossier? |
> | Positioning snapshot | In one short paragraph: who is Venkat for, when is he relevant, what value does he create, and why him? |
> | Best-fit audience and situation | Who benefits most? What situation or trigger makes the work necessary? |
> | Competitive alternatives | What would the buyer do instead—internal team, traditional consultancy, specialist vendor, fragmented execution, or nothing? |
> | Distinctive capabilities | Which capabilities does Venkat combine that the alternatives cannot credibly reproduce? |
> | Value and outcomes | What changes for the buyer because of those capabilities? Connect each capability to a consequential outcome. |
> | Category and differentiation | What useful category helps someone understand him? What is the strongest “onlyness” or remarkable distinction? |
> | Proof and confidence | Three to five concise reasons to believe, with each claim marked validated, supported, hypothesis, or unknown. |
> | Boundaries | What should Venkat not be called, hired for, or confused with? |
> | Maintenance and use | What documents derive from this one, what must not overwrite it, and what evidence triggers reconsideration? |
> 
> The Product Marketing Alliance’s practitioner template uses a similar core: concise description, category, competition, personas, unique differentiators, customer challenges, value, and regular review. It specifically maps each differentiator to the challenge it solves and the resulting value. [PMA positioning template](https://www.productmarketingalliance.com/product-positioning-template-framework/)
> 
> ## What it should not contain
> 
> Keep these elsewhere:
> 
> - Raw interview material and transcripts.
> - Long quotations.
> - Complete career history or biography.
> - Every case study or accomplishment.
> - Source-by-source analysis.
> - Claim-level line citations and source hashes.
> - The history of rejected interpretations.
> - Detailed market research and competitor profiles.
> - Messaging pillars, headlines, taglines, social copy, or elevator pitches.
> - Voice, tone, personality, or visual identity guidance.
> - Thought-leadership positions and beliefs.
> - Offer design, pricing, packaging, or delivery procedures.
> - Governance and implementation instructions.
> - Detailed Foundry-specific adaptations.
> - Unresolved hypotheses written as established truths.
> - Generic claims such as “innovative,” “strategic,” “transformational,” or “cross-functional” without a mechanism and proof.
> 
> Those may all be useful—but they belong in evidence, profile, point-of-view, messaging, voice, offer, or implementation documents.
> 
> The key distinction is:
> 
> - **Evidence dossier:** Why we believe the positioning.
> - **Positioning document:** What the current positioning is.
> - **Messaging document:** How we express it.
> - **Offer document:** What someone can buy.
> - **Profile document:** Who Venkat is and how to work with him.
> 
> ## Examples worth studying
> 
> ### 1. April Dunford — best strategic foundation
> 
> Her five-component model is the strongest foundation I found. Importantly, she warns against starting with a fill-in-the-blank statement before the underlying choices are established. [Official explanation](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/an-introduction-to-positioning)
> 
> For Venkat, this means we should not manufacture a polished positioning statement while the buyer, trigger, category, or offer remains unresolved.
> 
> ### 2. Geoffrey Moore — best final compression test
> 
> The classic structure is:
> 
> > For [target customer] who [need], [offering] is a [category] that [benefit]. Unlike [alternative], it [primary differentiation].
> 
> It is useful after the underlying position is supported—not as the research method. [Template and examples](https://www.smartsheet.com/value-proposition-positioning-templates)
> 
> ### 3. Marty Neumeier — best differentiation test
> 
> Neumeier’s “onlyness” test asks:
> 
> > Our brand is the only [category] that [compelling difference].
> 
> Examples include Cirque du Soleil as a circus with Broadway sophistication and JetBlue as business-class comfort at coach pricing. [Marty Neumeier’s official guide](https://www.martyneumeier.com/a-guide-to-agile-strategy)
> 
> For Venkat, this should be a qualification test, not permission to make an unsupported “only” claim.
>

>

> ### 5. Public Markdown examples
> 
> [Convox’s positioning file](https://github.com/rohansx/convox/blob/main/docs/positioning.md) leads with one-line and long-form positions, then explains alternatives and differentiated claims.
> 
> [Harbor’s positioning file](https://github.com/r/Harbor/blob/main/docs/POSITIONING.md) leads with a one-line summary, identifies what is missing in the market, explains differentiation, and states what Harbor is not.
> 
> Both are useful examples, but they also mix positioning with market research, messaging, and source material. They are examples to learn from—not templates to copy wholesale.
