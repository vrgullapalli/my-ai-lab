> # Contextual Voice Update Protocol
> 
> ## Purpose
> 
> This document explains how to turn voice discoveries from conversations into reliable writing guidance without turning every correction into a universal rule.
> 
> The goal is not to make Venkat sound identical everywhere.
> 
> The goal is:
> 
> > **The same person thinking naturally in different rooms.**
> 
> The stable identity should remain recognizable. The expression should change with the format, audience, purpose, source material, relationship, and stakes.
> 
> ---
> 
> ## The central rule
> 
> Treat conversation corrections as **evidence about the voice**, not automatic instructions for every future piece.
> 
> Every meaningful correction must answer two questions:
> 
> 1. **What does this reveal about Venkat's voice?**
> 2. **Under what conditions is it true?**
> 
> A correction should become a global rule only when Venkat states that it always applies or when it is repeatedly supported across different contexts.
> 
> ---
> 
> ## What counts as useful voice evidence
> 
> Capture a voice update when Venkat:
> 
> - rewrites a sentence and says his version sounds more like him;
> - rejects wording as stiff, polished, corporate, academic, generic, or AI-like;
> - explains why a phrase changes his meaning;
> - approves an example as representative of his natural voice;
> - distinguishes how he would speak to different readers;
> - corrects rhythm, paragraphing, tone, certainty, warmth, or level of detail;
> - says a behavior should always or never occur;
> - changes an earlier voice decision.
> 
> Not every edit is a voice update. Some edits correct the idea, facts, positioning, or strategy. Keep those separate.
> 
> ---
> 
> ## The update workflow
> 
> ### 1. Capture the correction
> 
> Preserve enough of the exchange to understand what changed:
> 
> - original wording;
> - Venkat's correction;
> - why he rejected or changed it;
> - the version he approved, if one exists.
> 
> Do not save only the polished final sentence. The contrast often contains the most useful evidence.
> 
> ### 2. Identify what actually changed
> 
> Classify the correction before calling it a voice rule:
> 
> - **Meaning:** The first version said the wrong thing.
> - **Positioning:** It implied the wrong role, expertise, or relationship.
> - **Reasoning:** It hid, shortened, or distorted the thought process.
> - **Voice:** It did not sound like Venkat.
> - **Mechanics:** Rhythm, punctuation, sentence length, paragraphing, or grammar was wrong.
> - **Context:** The wording was reasonable elsewhere but wrong for this reader, format, or purpose.
> 
> One correction can belong to more than one category.
> 
> ### 3. Record the context
> 
> Attach the conditions that shaped the correction:
> 
> | Context field | Question |
> |---|---|
> | Format | Article, LinkedIn post, website, presentation, email, prompt, or another form? |
> | Audience | Who is reading, and what do they already know? |
> | Relationship | Stranger, client, peer, colleague, friend, or existing reader? |
> | Purpose | Explain, challenge, recommend, reflect, persuade, teach, or respond? |
> | Source condition | Is the thinking settled, exploratory, emotional, researched, or incomplete? |
> | Stakes | Casual, public, reputational, strategic, executive, legal, or regulated? |
> | Evidence burden | Personal observation, supported claim, or decision-grade argument? |
> 
> If the correction cannot be understood without these conditions, it is contextual.
> 
> ### 4. Assign an authority level
> 
> #### Hard constraint
> 
> Applies everywhere unless Venkat explicitly changes it.
> 
> Examples:
> 
> - Do not use em dashes.
> - Do not invent facts, stories, numbers, or quotations.
> - Do not silently change settled meaning.
> 
> #### Stable tendency
> 
> Usually reflects Venkat's voice but should not be forced into every piece.
> 
> Examples:
> 
> - Let the reader see the thinking develop.
> - Prefer plain language over professional-sounding abstraction.
> - Speak with the reader rather than presenting at them.
> - Preserve useful human texture instead of polishing every seam away.
> 
> #### Contextual tendency
> 
> Applies when named conditions are present.
> 
> Examples:
> 
> - In reflective long-form writing, first person can carry discovery and changed judgment.
> - For senior leaders, raise decision relevance and evidence discipline without becoming corporate.
> - In a note to colleagues, shared context and an unfinished edge may remain.
> 
> #### Optional move
> 
> Part of the available voice, but only when the material earns it.
> 
> Examples:
> 
> - dry wit;
> - mild irreverence;
> - rhetorical questions;
> - fragments;
> - visible self-correction;
> - analogy;
> - personal story.
> 
> #### Calibration example
> 
> An approved example that teaches rhythm, judgment, or thought movement. It is not reusable language and does not create a rule by itself.
> 
> ### 5. Set the evidence status
> 
> Use one of these labels:
> 
> - **EXPLICIT:** Venkat stated the rule or preference directly.
> - **OBSERVED:** It appears in his real speech, writing, or corrections.
> - **SUPPORTED INFERENCE:** Several observations support the pattern.
> - **HYPOTHESIS:** Plausible but not yet reliable.
> - **UNKNOWN:** The available material does not answer it.
> 
> The latest explicit correction outranks an older pattern.
> 
> ### 6. Route the update to the narrowest useful location
> 
> | Type of update | Destination |
> |---|---|
> | Always or never | Global constraints |
> | Recognizable way of thinking or relating | Core voice principles |
> | Sentence, paragraph, punctuation, or rhythm | Mechanics |
> | Changes with reader or purpose | Context profile or voice dials |
> | Applies to one surface | Website, article, LinkedIn, presentation, email, or other format profile |
> | Shows a strong approved contrast | Calibration examples |
> | Changes role or professional identity | Positioning context, not the voice guide |
> | Changes the substance of an argument | Content or reasoning system, not the voice guide |
> 
> Use the narrowest location that captures the lesson. Do not repeat the same rule across several files unless each location needs a different operational version.
> 
> ### 7. Resolve conflicts
> 
> Use this order:
> 
> 1. Latest explicit correction
> 2. Current surface-specific specification
> 3. Current global voice guidance
> 4. Repeated observed patterns
> 5. Inference or hypothesis
> 
> When two rules appear to conflict, first ask whether they apply to different contexts. A website-specific ban does not automatically become a global ban.
> 
> If the conflict would materially change the result and context does not resolve it, ask Venkat. Do not silently choose.
> 
> ### 8. Preserve the evidence
> 
> For every meaningful update, retain:
> 
> - the decision record;
> - the before-and-after example;
> - the context metadata;
> - the authority and evidence status;
> - the rule it replaced, if any;
> - the date of the correction.
> 
> This creates traceability. Future agents can see not just the rule, but why it exists and when it applies.
> 
> ### 9. Test the update
> 
> Test the rule against at least two contexts:
> 
> 1. the context that produced the correction;
> 2. a different context where it might not apply.
> 
> Ask:
> 
> - Does the rule improve resemblance in the original context?
> - Does it distort the voice elsewhere?
> - Does it preserve the idea and evidence boundaries?
> - Does it make the writing sound more like Venkat or merely more stylized?
> 
> The goal is **resemblance without caricature**.
> 
> ### 10. Promote, narrow, replace, or retire
> 
> As more evidence appears:
> 
> - **Promote** a repeated contextual pattern to a stable tendency.
> - **Narrow** a rule that was applied too broadly.
> - **Replace** an older rule when Venkat explicitly corrects it.
> - **Retire** guidance that no longer reflects his voice.
> - **Keep unresolved** anything the evidence does not settle.
> 
> Do not continually append rules. Maintain one current rule and preserve prior decisions in the calibration history.
>

> ## Instructions for an AI agent
> 
> When reviewing a conversation for voice updates:
> 
> 1. Extract only explicit corrections, approved examples, repeated patterns, and meaningful rejections.
> 2. Separate voice changes from changes to meaning, facts, positioning, reasoning, and strategy.
> 3. Record the context that produced each correction.
> 4. Assign an authority level and evidence status.
> 5. Prefer a contextual rule over a global rule unless the evidence supports global use.
> 6. Compare each update with the current guide and identify whether it confirms, refines, conflicts with, or replaces existing guidance.
> 7. Update the narrowest relevant section.
> 8. Preserve approved before-and-after examples as calibration evidence.
> 9. Flag unresolved conflicts instead of silently choosing.
> 10. Return a change log showing what changed, why, where it applies, and what it replaced.
> 
> Do not imitate surface quirks merely because they appear in the source. Do not force questions, stories, fragments, humor, analogies, contradictions, reframes, or non-obvious conclusions. Voice changes expression. It does not determine the idea.
> 
> ---
> 
> ## Final quality check
> 
> Before accepting a voice update, ask:
> 
> - Is this really about voice?
> - Is the context recorded?
> - Is the authority supported by evidence?
> - Did we make it broader than Venkat intended?
> - Does it conflict with a newer correction?
> - Is it stored in the narrowest useful place?
> - Did we preserve a real example?
> - Does the update improve resemblance without creating a caricature?
> - Would the same person still be recognizable in a different room?
