## What the research says goes in one

Consistent across Mailchimp, Glean, and the AI-specific writers:

- **Voice vs. tone split.** Voice never changes. Tone changes by situation. Mailchimp makes this the first section.
- **3 to 5 principles**, not twenty. Each one stated as a rule you can check.
- **Banned and preferred words.** The single most useful section for an AI.
- **Structural rules.** Sentence length, paragraph length, headings, list format, punctuation.
- **Tone by situation.** A table of contexts, not a paragraph.
- **Annotated samples.** Two or three, with notes on why each line works.
- The strongest advice: **be more explicit and more example-heavy than you would be for a human.** Abstract words like "punchy" mean nothing to a model.

## The part most guides miss

Mia Kiraki's May 2026 piece is the sharpest thing I found, and you already have it on disk. Her argument:

- A voice profile inventories **what** you do. Your voice is an engine that decides **why**. Inventories can't hold engines.
- **What you delete matters more than what you keep.** She calls it rejection architecture. Trace each ban back to where the allergy came from, so the model has a reason instead of a rule.
- **Drift is structural.** Your rules are strong for the first few hundred words and fade after roughly 800. Say that in the doc instead of pretending it isn't true.
- Three sections she added under the inventory: rejection architecture, contextual shift logic, and **judgment calls the AI will get wrong**.
- Her honest ceiling: "pretty solid." The doc's real jobs are editing compass, drift detector, and quality gate. Not a clone.

## Sections I'd use

1. **How to use this file** — three lines. Name the piece type first, then apply.
2. **The floor** — rules that never bend. From v2.
3. **Rules that depend** — a table. Rule, fires when, skip when.
4. **Piece types** — a table. Type, example, what turns on.
5. **Never** — banned words and moves, each with the reason it's banned.
6. **How the voice shifts** — by audience and stakes, and why.
7. **Where AI will get it wrong** — named decision points, with your choice next to the model's likely choice.
8. **Evidence** — the observed patterns from the transcript work, with counts, marked Observed.
9. **Samples** — two or three real passages, annotated.
10. **Check before publishing** — the ordered questions from v2.
11. **Drift note and changelog** — dated changes only, never silent edits.

## Format conventions

- Tables for anything conditional. Prose for nothing.
- Every rule gets a trigger. A rule with no trigger gets applied everywhere and ruins things.
- Show, don't describe. Bad line, then good line, side by side.
- Label every claim Provided / Observed / Inferred, same as the rest of this repo.
- Keep it under about 300 lines. Past that the model stops honoring the back half.
- Date every change at the bottom.

## Three things I'd ask you before anyone writes it

1. **Is this one file or two?** Your written voice and your spoken voice are measurably different in the evidence pack. One file may be trying to hold two things.
2. **Who reads it?** Only me, or also the skills and agents? That changes whether it's a rule file or a context file.
3. **Do the transcript findings go in, or stay separate?** The evidence pack is Observed. The style doc is mostly Provided. Mixing them without labels is how a preference turns into a fact.

- `[GUIDE: ...]` — instructions to you. When to use the section, how to adapt it, when to cut it. Deleted before the file goes live.

- `[EXAMPLE: ...]` — filler showing the shape. Not your content.
