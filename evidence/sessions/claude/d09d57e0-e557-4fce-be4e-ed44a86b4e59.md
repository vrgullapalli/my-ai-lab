## The Substack draft (`18-substack-draft.md`)

**Can Your Company Follow One Customer?**
*A ten-second test for whether the AI you bought can be trusted with the next move.*

A brand I worked with had six websites. The person tagging them knew the tools. Google Analytics, Tag Manager, all of it. He tagged each site the way he thought best.

Then we pulled the data from all six together, and I couldn't tell what was happening. Events meant different things on every site. One site was built for patients, another for physicians, and nothing shared a naming convention. Six sets of clean numbers that could not add up to one customer.

Nobody did anything wrong. He knew the tools. The standards were missing.

I think about that brand every time someone shows me a next best action program.

**The test**

Here is the question I ask, and it takes ten seconds:

**Can you follow one person across your channels and tell a true story about what they did? End to end. And have it hold. And then can you repeat that?**

Not "do you have the data." Not "can we see the dashboard." One person, one true story, across email, web, field, and media. If the story holds, and holds again next month, your company can trust a recommendation. If it doesn't, no recommendation engine can save you, because the engine reads the same broken story you do.

That is the whole argument. The rest of this piece is what to do about it.

**The engines are ready. That part is real.**

I want to be clear about something: the AI is good now, and it is getting better fast.

Google launched Ask Advisor this May. It spans Ads and Analytics, explains performance, recommends next steps, and waits for your approval before changes ship. Veeva is putting AI agents inside its CRM, and its own design tells you what matters: the agents sit on top of a unified customer database. Salesforce announced in April that a major biotech is consolidating its systems into one customer view, with next best action as the goal on top. Sanofi says its Turing system already recommends what to communicate, when, and through which channel.

Read that list again and notice the pattern. The companies running this at scale, and the vendors building the engines, all put the unified view first and the recommendation on top of it. That order is the lesson.

**The honest number**

IQVIA surveyed 107 senior commercial leaders in life sciences last year. More than 80 percent said their organizations were past AI pilots. Eleven percent said their data was fully sufficient for commercial AI.

Nine in ten stop short of calling their data ready. If that is you, you are not behind your peers. You are standing with almost all of them. The window is still open, and the path is known.

**What the recommendation has to know**

For a next best action to mean anything, the system picking it needs answers to questions like these. Who is this customer? What has already happened to them, in every channel, not one? Where are they in their journey? What did we do last time, and what happened after?

Answering those takes a layer of work that has a shape. This is the order I would run it in, because I have run it:

1. **One taxonomy.** Every asset, message, and event named one way, everywhere, tied to what it is for.
2. **One identity.** One customer, one record. If the same person lives under three IDs, every count you have is fiction.
3. **Scored engagement.** Interactions scored and assigned to the individual, not to the campaign.
4. **One unified profile.** The channels, the scores, the journey state, and for a healthcare customer, how they actually behave clinically, together in one place.
5. **The authority to keep it that way.** Definitions mean one thing. Someone owns each one. Vendors and teams follow the standard or the standard dies.

Here is what I have seen that layer pay, from my own records: monthly reporting that took weeks came down by 80 to 90 percent, and the approach we built for one account scaled to sixty. Those are my numbers from my own engagements, and I label them that way on purpose. In 2020 I designed and led an audit that mapped over 150 marketing assets against the customer journey and scored every one of them. That audit became a taxonomy, a measurement routine, and a governance model the client kept using. The foundation work pays for itself before any AI shows up.

A note on buying versus owning. A vendor can absolutely build the plumbing. The leaders hire platforms to consolidate their systems, and that works. What cannot be outsourced is ownership: your definitions, your identity rules, your journey logic, and the authority to enforce them. When those live with whoever built the last dashboard, you don't have a context layer. You have someone else's opinion of your business.

**The part nobody budgets for**

Go read the omnichannel job postings in pharma right now. There is a pattern in them. Companies are hiring one director to own the AI decision engines, the marketing automation, the campaign strategy, and the vendor stack, while the data foundation that all of it depends on belongs to a different team that this person does not control.

The mandate is bigger than the authority. I have lived on both sides of that arrangement, and I can tell you where it goes: the person delivers dashboards, because dashboards are what you can deliver without authority.

So if you are the person these initiatives roll up to, here is the question that decides more than any platform choice: **does the person you hired to own next best action have the authority to enforce one taxonomy, one identity, and one set of definitions across every team and vendor that touches the customer?**

I am not going to pretend there is one right org chart. There isn't. But that question has a yes or a no, and everything downstream depends on it.

**Score your own company this week**

Six questions. Yes, partial, or no.

1. Do we have one taxonomy for content, events, and campaigns across every channel and vendor?
2. Does one customer exist as one record, everywhere?
3. Is engagement scored and assigned to the individual?
4. Are journey stages defined, agreed, and actually tagged in the data?
5. Does every key metric have exactly one definition and one owner?
6. Do we record what happened after a recommendation, and does anything learn from it?

Take it to your team as it is. Forward it to whoever needs to see it. You don't need me for any of it, and that is the point.

Then, if you want the ten-second version: ask the AI you already pay for to follow one customer across your channels and tell you a true story. One warning. A fluent answer is not a passing answer. The story has to hold against what you know actually happened, and it has to hold twice.

If you run the six questions and want to compare notes on what your team answered, my inbox is open. I have seen a lot of these scorecards, and the patterns are more encouraging than you might expect.
