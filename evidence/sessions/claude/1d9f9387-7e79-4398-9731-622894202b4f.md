> PROOF AND VERIFICATION MODEL
> 
> PURPOSE
> 
> Proof-to-Market does not treat a polished artifact, output, case study, repository,
> Skill, agent, testimonial, credential, or process narrative as proof by default.
> 
> As AI makes outputs, explanations, demonstrations, and representations of process
> increasingly easy to produce, evaluate proof through the relationship between:
> 
> claim -> evidence -> provenance -> verification -> consequence -> limits
> 
> The goal of proof is to reduce justified uncertainty about a claim.
> 
> The goal of a proof asset is to preserve and expose enough of this relationship
> for another party to inspect, test, challenge, reproduce, or otherwise evaluate
> the claim without relying primarily on Venkat's assertion.
> 
> 
> CORE DEFINITIONS
> 
> Claim
> A bounded assertion being evaluated.
> 
> Evidence
> Observable material that bears on the claim.
> 
> Verification
> The act or mechanism used to test the claim against the available evidence.
> 
> Proof
> The justified conclusion supported by the evidence and verification available
> at that point in time. Proof is claim-relative and evidence-relative. It is not
> an intrinsic property of an artifact.
> 
> Proof Asset
> A reusable, inspectable verification object that connects a bounded claim to
> evidence from real work and preserves enough provenance, verification,
> consequence, and limitation for another party to evaluate the claim.
> 
> Proof Portfolio
> A governed collection of proof assets that, together, support a broader capability
> or set of claims.
> 
> 
> IMPORTANT DISTINCTIONS
> 
> Do not equate:
> - artifact with proof
> - evidence with proof
> - provenance with validity
> - execution with capability
> - one successful case with general reliability
> - a convincing explanation with verified reasoning
> - an executable object with evidence that it works
> - AI-generated evaluation with independent verification by default
> 
> A GitHub repository might contain proof.
> A Claude Skill might expose proof.
> A case study might communicate proof.
> None qualifies as proof merely because it exists.
> 
> 
> VERIFICATION ACROSS THE WORK LIFECYCLE
> 
> Do not impose a boundary between verification during work and verification after work.
> 
> Verification follows the work.
> 
> During execution, work should produce and use appropriate verification evidence:
> sources, observations, assumptions, decisions, alternatives, actions, checks,
> failures, corrections, approvals, results, and execution traces.
> 
> After execution, retained evidence should support later verification questions
> about outcomes, judgment, capability, reliability, learning, and other claims.
> 
> The same evidence substrate may support several later verification uses.
> 
> Do not require the future use of every piece of evidence to be known when the
> evidence is generated.
> 
> 
> PROOF CATEGORIES
> 
> Use these as current working categories, not a closed taxonomy:
> 
> 1. Provenance proof
>    Helps establish where evidence came from and what occurred.
> 
> 2. Execution proof
>    Helps establish that work was performed as represented.
> 
> 3. Judgment proof
>    Helps establish the quality of reasoning, choices, tradeoffs, corrections,
>    or decisions under uncertainty.
> 
> 4. Capability proof
>    Helps establish that a person, system, human-AI combination, method, or
>    workflow possesses a defined capability.
> 
> 5. Outcome proof
>    Helps establish that work produced a claimed consequence.
> 
> 6. Reliability and assurance proof
>    Helps establish that a capability survives repeated use, challenge, failure,
>    controls, recovery, and relevant operating conditions.
> 
> 7. Learning proof
>    Helps establish that failures, corrections, or new evidence changed later
>    behavior or performance.
> 
> One proof asset may support several categories.
> 
> Do not force evidence into one category when several relationships are material.
> Create a new category when a genuinely different verification question requires one.
> 
> 
> EVIDENCE STRENGTH
> 
> Describe why evidence is strong rather than assigning confidence from appearance.
> 
> Relevant evidence properties include:
> 
> - Direct: comes from the work itself.
> - Corroborated: supported by independent evidence sources.
> - Evaluated: tested against explicit criteria.
> - Challenged: subjected to an independent or adversarial attempt to disconfirm it.
> - Reproduced: produced a comparable result again.
> - Comparative: assessed against a baseline, alternative, or counterfactual.
> - Longitudinal: supported across time or multiple materially different cases.
> - Consequential: someone relied on the result for a real decision, action,
>   responsibility, commitment, or other meaningful consequence.
> 
> These properties are composable.
> 
> Do not assume every claim requires every property.
> 
> Do not hide missing forms of verification.
> 
> Do not turn strength into a single numeric score unless a defined use case
> genuinely requires one and the score preserves the important distinctions.
> 
> 
> PROOF ASSET CONTRACT
> 
> For every proposed proof asset, identify:
> 
> 1. Subject
>    What person, system, method, workflow, human-AI combination, or outcome is
>    being evaluated?
> 
> 2. Claim
>    What exact assertion is the asset meant to help verify?
> 
> 3. Decision supported
>    What uncertainty or decision does verification of this claim help resolve?
> 
> 4. Evidence
>    What material supports or contradicts the claim?
> 
> 5. Provenance
>    Where did the evidence originate, under what conditions, and when?
> 
> 6. Verification method
>    How was or will the claim be tested, challenged, inspected, reproduced,
>    compared, or evaluated?
> 
> 7. Verification result
>    What did the available verification establish?
> 
> 8. Strength basis
>    Which evidence-strength properties are present and absent?
> 
> 9. Limits
>    What does the evidence not establish?
> 
> 10. Freshness
>     Under what conditions does the proof become stale or require reverification?
> 
> 11. Exposure
>     What evidence may be public, restricted, summarized, sanitized, or withheld?
> 
> 12. Reuse
>     Which other claims, situations, audiences, or decisions may legitimately
>     use the same evidence without changing what the evidence supports?
> 
> 13. Version and history
>     What new evidence, failure, correction, reproduction, or evaluation has

> - decision proof
> - case proof
> - evaluation or benchmark

>

>    uncertainty stands in the way.
> 
> 2. Identify the smallest meaningful claim whose verification would reduce that

>    that work from the beginning.
> 
> 8. Preserve the resulting evidence and verification history so later uses do
>    not restart from zero.
> 
> 9. Present only the evidence relevant to the recipient's decision while
>    preserving the underlying claim, provenance, limits, and uncertainty.
> 
> 10. Update the proof asset when new successes, failures, corrections,
>     reproductions, external consequences, or evaluations materially change what
>     is supported.
> 
> 
> REVENUE BOUNDARY
> 
> Proof-to-Market uses proof to support market decisions.
> 
> Market attractiveness, persuasion, buyer interest, or revenue potential does
> not determine whether something qualifies as proof.
> 
> The market situation determines:
> - which uncertainty matters
> - which claim matters
> - what depth of verification is useful
> - which existing proof asset is relevant
> - whether closing a proof gap is worth the effort
> 
> The proof model determines:
> - what the evidence supports
> - how the claim was verified
> - how strong the support is
> - what remains unknown
> - what must not be claimed
> 
> Never strengthen a claim because a stronger claim would sell better.
> 
> 
> PROGRESSIVE VERIFICATION
> 
> Where useful, design proof assets so verification works at several depths.
> 
> A recipient should be able to move from:
> 
> understand the claim
> -> inspect the important evidence
> -> examine the verification
> -> follow provenance
> -> challenge assumptions and limits
> -> reproduce or interact with the capability where appropriate
> 
> Do not require every recipient to inspect every layer.
> 
> Make deeper verification available when the consequence warrants it.
> 
> 
> LIVING PROOF
> 
> Treat proof assets as potentially living objects.
> 
> New evidence may strengthen them.
> Failures may weaken them.
> Corrections may change their boundaries.
> Reproduction may expand what is supported.
> Staleness may reduce confidence.
> New operating conditions may require reverification.
> 
> Never freeze a favorable conclusion while ignoring later contradictory evidence.
> 
> 
> FINAL TEST
> 
> Before calling anything a proof asset, ask:
> 
> "If the creator's explanation disappeared, would the remaining evidence,
> provenance, verification, consequence, and limits still give a reasonable
> verifier a meaningful basis for evaluating the claim?"
> 
> If no, it is not yet a sufficient proof asset.
