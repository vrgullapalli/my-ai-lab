The conversation explored **how to build a content graph for content marketing**—a map showing how your ideas, audience problems, evidence, and content connect.

- **Your starting point:** You already have your ideal customer profile (ICP), overarching content strategy, pillars, and topics.
- **What to add:** Core ideas you want to be known for, connected to audience problems, proof, stories, examples, and relevant tools or offers.
- **The main shift:** Put **core ideas at the center**, rather than topics. Topics define what you discuss; core ideas express your point of view and help build recognition.

The proposed workflow had three phases:

1. **Extract:** Review existing material. Ask: What am I noticing? What’s the tension? What assumption sits underneath it? What do I believe instead? Capture 30–50 raw ideas without judging them.
2. **Organize:** Group similar ideas and select 8–12 core beliefs. Test whether each is **true, useful, distinctly yours, and provable**. Connect them to your ICP’s problems, pillars, and supporting evidence.
3. **Activate:** Ask where each belief changes a decision. Turn those applications into posts and articles, connect them in your content map, and refine the map based on audience response.

**Next step:** Try the extraction process on one existing piece of your work. Start with a simple thinking map before building a technical graph.

A **core idea is a belief you want your audience to associate with you**. It explains how you see a problem and what you think people should do about it.

For example:

- **Pillar:** Customer data strategy.
- **Topic:** Data quality.
- **Core idea:** “Data quality priorities should start with the decisions the data needs to support.”

You already have your ICP, pillars, and topics. Use them as starting points for the following process. **Start with one pillar and develop 3–5 candidate ideas**; you don’t need to find all your core ideas at once.

**1. Identify the problems your audience actually encounters**

Pick one pillar. List the situations where your ICP gets stuck, wastes effort, or struggles to make a decision. Use customer conversations, questions, and observations wherever possible.

Use this prompt:

> Here is my ICP: [paste].  
> Here is one content pillar: [paste].  
> Here are customer questions, notes, or observations: [paste].  
> Identify the recurring problems connected to this pillar. Describe each as a specific situation, using the customer’s language where available. Separate documented problems from inferred ones, and flag anything that needs validation.

**What you should have:** A short list of concrete problems, such as “Teams keep fixing data issues but cannot explain which fixes matter most.”

**2. Extract what you have noticed about those problems**

Review your experience: projects, conversations, posts, notes, successes, and failures. Look for patterns you keep explaining to people.

Ask yourself:

- What keeps happening?
- What do people usually try?
- Why does that approach sometimes fall short?
- What have I seen work, and under what conditions?
- What advice do I find myself repeating?

Use this prompt:

> Review these materials: [paste].  
> Extract observations that could explain the audience problems we identified. For each, show the observation, the passage or example supporting it, and the possible implication. Preserve my meaning. Don’t invent experiences or turn tentative observations into established facts.

**What you should have:** Raw observations—for example, “Teams prioritize the easiest data errors to count, even when other errors affect more important decisions.”

**3. Turn observations into candidate beliefs**

Now ask: **What do I believe someone should understand or do differently because of this?**

Try this sentence:

> When [audience] faces [problem], I believe they should [approach], because [reason].

For example:

> When teams prioritize data fixes, I believe they should start with the business decisions affected, because error counts alone don’t show business impact.

Use this prompt:

> Turn these observations into candidate core ideas. For each, state:
> - The audience problem.
> - The belief in one plain sentence.
> - The reasoning behind it.
> - What someone would do differently if they accepted it.
>
> Stay faithful to my observations. Flag missing reasoning and ask me questions instead of filling gaps with generic advice. Don’t force a contrarian position.

**What you should have:** A set of clear beliefs, each with a practical consequence.

**4. Test whether you can stand behind each belief**

A catchy sentence is only a candidate. Check its substance.

Use this prompt:

> Help me stress-test these candidate beliefs: [paste].  
> For each, ask:
> - What evidence or experience supports it?
> - When might it be wrong or less useful?
> - Is it specific enough to guide a decision?
> - Do I actually believe this, or does it merely sound appealing?
> - What qualification would make it more accurate?
>
> Label each as supported, partly supported, or a hypothesis based only on the material provided. Suggest more precise wording where needed.

**What you should have:** Credible, appropriately scoped beliefs. A hypothesis can become something you investigate; it doesn’t have to become a public claim yet.

**5. Choose the ideas you want to return to**

Group overlapping beliefs. Choose a small starting set based on:

- **Relevance:** Your ICP cares about the problem.
- **Conviction:** You can explain why you believe it.
- **Support:** You have evidence or examples.
- **Usefulness:** It changes a decision or action.
- **Depth:** You can explore it through several different situations.

Use this prompt:

> Group these beliefs by their underlying idea: [paste].  
> Distinguish the main beliefs from supporting points and examples. Suggest 3–5 core ideas to develop first, using audience relevance, my conviction, available support, practical usefulness, and depth. Explain the tradeoffs. Identify what I need to decide myself.

You don’t need a belief nobody has ever expressed. Your experience, reasoning, and examples can make a familiar idea recognizably yours.

**6. Make a simple card for each chosen idea**

Use this template:

> **Core idea:** What do I believe?  
> **Audience problem:** Who needs this, and in what situation?  
> **Reasoning:** Why do I believe it?  
> **Support:** What examples, observations, or evidence support it?  
> **Limits:** When does it not apply?  
> **Action:** What should someone do differently?  
> **Pillar:** Where does it fit?

Then test its depth:

> Using this core idea card, suggest five distinct content angles: an explanation, a practical walkthrough, an example, an objection, and a boundary case. Each should help the audience understand or apply the belief differently. Flag any angle that requires evidence I haven’t supplied.

**What you should have:** A usable foundation for connected content. Publish and discuss the ideas, then refine them based on the questions, objections, and examples people bring back.

**Your first task:** Pick one pillar and write down three audience problems plus three things you’ve noticed about them. That is enough to begin.
