**Recommended**

Use an **Asset-Use Fit** methodology.

It’s the closest appropriate equivalent to product-market fit because an intellectual asset earns fit through use, not merely attention.

**Method**

Track this behavioral sequence:

> Recognize → Save → Retrieve → Apply → Benefit → Share → Return → Request more

Each behavior answers a different fit question.

| Behavior | What it tells us | Likely motivation |
|---|---|---|
| **Recognize** | The problem feels relevant | “This describes what I’m experiencing.” |
| **Save** | The asset has anticipated future value | “I’ll need this in a meeting or decision later.” |
| **Retrieve** | The asset remained memorable and useful | “This is the thing that could help me now.” |
| **Apply** | The form works in real life | “I can use this without excessive effort.” |
| **Benefit** | It changes understanding, action, or a decision | “This helped me see, decide, or do something better.” |
| **Share** | It creates value for another person or group | “My colleague needs this,” or “This will help us align.” |
| **Return** | Its value continues or compounds | “I want to use this again with new evidence.” |
| **Request more** | There may be commercial pull | “I need the deeper version, guided help, or another asset.” |

**Save fit**

Saving is evidence of anticipated value, not proven value.

People may save an article or post because:

- It names a problem they expect to face.
- It provides language they want for a future meeting.
- It contains a checklist, question, diagram, or test worth referencing.
- It makes a complicated idea easier to remember.
- It protects against forgetting something consequential.
- It reflects the kind of leader or practitioner they want to become.
- They don’t have time to act now but don’t want to lose it.

The stronger evidence comes later: did they retrieve and use what they saved?

**Share fit**

Sharing is different from saving.

People may share because:

- It helps a colleague.
- It explains something they’ve struggled to articulate.
- It creates a neutral way to raise a difficult concern.
- It helps align business, data, technology, and agency teams.
- It makes the sender appear thoughtful, informed, or useful.
- It reinforces professional identity.
- It gives the group a common question, model, or decision language.

The strongest share evidence isn’t the share itself. It’s whether the recipient recognizes, uses, or forwards the asset again.

**Fit levels**

**Problem fit**

The intended user recognizes the problem as real and important.

**Form fit**

The asset appears in the right format, length, language, and moment. People understand it and believe it is worth saving.

**Use fit**

People retrieve and apply it without needing Venkat to explain the unstated logic.

**Outcome fit**

Use produces clearer understanding, a better question, a changed action, or an improved decision.

**Transfer fit**

The asset works for another person, team, brand, or situation without losing its meaning.

**Continuity fit**

People return to it, reuse it, or contribute new evidence that improves it.

**Market fit**

People actively seek it, request deeper help, recommend it, return repeatedly, or demonstrate willingness to pay.

**Important boundary**

A high save or share rate does not establish Asset-Use Fit.

A post can be widely saved because it sounds insightful but never be retrieved. It can be shared because it signals identity but never improve a decision.

The strongest proof sequence is:

> Saved → retrieved → applied → helped → shared through use → used again

**Asset maturity**

Keep maturity and fit separate:

- **Candidate:** The asset is coherent enough to examine.
- **Developed:** It works across more than one relevant case.
- **Applied:** Someone has used it in real work.
- **Supported:** Repeated use shows where it helps and where it doesn’t.

An asset can be well developed internally without market fit. It can also receive attention before the underlying method is trustworthy.

Seth Godin’s Purple Cow principle is not “make it louder” or “make it strange.”

It means creating something a specific audience considers worth noticing and talking about. The audience, not the creator, decides whether it is remarkable. The correct name is **Decision-Grade Evidence Test**.

It does not determine whether the organization’s data is universally correct.

It helps an accountable leader determine:

- What decision the result is being used to support.
- What the number claims to represent.
- Whether the business question, definition, data, method, or context changed.
- Whether the difference is legitimate, explainable, or unresolved.
- What uncertainty could materially change the decision.
- Whether to use the result, qualify it, verify something specific, revise the measure, or pause.

Its non-obvious distinction is:

> A number can be correct and still be unsafe for the decision.

That is more remarkable than “improve reporting quality.”

Use a **Remarkable Asset Fit** method.

- Evidence and problem fit
- Asset-Use Fit

- Defensibility and compounding value

## **Minimum threshold**

A candidate enters the asset pool only if every requirement passes.

1. **Real evidence**

   It begins with a real problem, episode, recurring decision, or observed behavior.

2. **Specific user**

   We know who encounters it and the moment when it matters.

3. **Useful job**

   The asset helps the user understand, decide, act, explain, or preserve something important.

4. **Non-obvious distinction**

   It reveals something true and consequential that the conventional framing misses.

5. **Remark sentence**

   We can write the sentence a user might repeat to someone else.

   For this candidate:

   > “The number may be correct, but that doesn’t mean it’s safe for the decision.”

6. **Behavior motive**

   We can explain why someone would save, download, share, or use it.

7. **Immediate test**

   A small real-world test could prove the asset useful or expose its weakness.

8. **Responsible boundary**

   It doesn’t invent evidence, overclaim root cause, expose private material, or automate consequential judgment prematurely.

This is a hard gate. A high aggregate score cannot compensate for failing one of these requirements.

After testing, score the candidate out of 100:

| Area | Weight |
|---|---:|
| **Problem and market fit** | 20 |

| **User experience and actual use** | 15 |
| **Purple Cow remarkability** | 10 |
| **Save, download, share, and relevant viral behavior** | 15 |
| **Defensibility and compounding value** | 10 |
| **Feasibility, maintenance, and premature-build risk** | 10 |

- At least **80/100**.

# Complete delivery process

## Phase 1: Establish the truth of the problem

### 1. Reconstruct the originating work

Document the actual experience:

- What happened?
- Who encountered it?
- What decision was being made?
- What failed?
- What did people do instead?
- What consequences followed?
- What remains unknown?

**Output:** Evidence-backed problem reconstruction.

### 2. Identify the recurring mechanism

Move beneath “reports were inconsistent” to identify what repeatedly produced the problem:

- inconsistent definitions
- fragmented tagging
- local calculations
- missing ownership
- undocumented transformations
- brand-level autonomy
- correction chains
- decisions continuing despite uncertainty

**Output:** A concise mechanism statement explaining why the problem recurs.

### 3. Define the target user and moment

Specify:

- Who experiences this?
- When does it become urgent?
- What are they trying to decide?
- What can they reasonably know?
- What would meaningful progress look like?

**Output:** Audience, trigger, decision, and desired outcome.

---

## Phase 2: Select the first asset

### 4. Create a comparable candidate card for every idea

Each candidate receives the same fields:

- problem addressed
- intended user
- moment of use
- core experience
- result produced
- reason to save
- reason to download
- reason to share
- reason to return
- evidence required
- economic path
- likely failure condition

**Output:** Twelve directly comparable candidate cards.

### 5. Apply the minimum threshold

A candidate cannot proceed unless it has:

- a recognizable and consequential problem
- a specific user and use moment
- a distinct, non-obvious insight
- immediate practical value
- a plausible reason to save or use it
- a remarkable premise under Purple Cow principles
- enough evidence to build responsibly
- a plausible path to continued or economic value

If it fails one of the essential conditions, it stops.

**Output:** Passed, revise, or rejected classification.

### 6. Apply the decision-break evidence test

For every candidate, ask:

> What evidence would make us decide not to build this?

Examples:

- Users understand the problem but wouldn’t use the asset.
- The asset describes something interesting but changes no decision.
- The insight feels obvious to the intended audience.
- People say they would save it but cannot name when they would retrieve it.
- Sharing would expose or embarrass the person’s organization.
- The experience requires unavailable or confidential data.
- A simpler existing tool already solves the job.

**Output:** Explicit evidence that could kill or materially change each candidate.

### 7. Compare candidates without averaging away fatal weaknesses

Score:

- **Market fit**
- **Value**
- **User experience**
- **Purple Cow**
- **Saveability**
- **Downloadability**
- **Shareability**
- **Virality**
- **Defensibility**
- **Asset-use fit**

Purple Cow and real user value are gates, not bonus points. A high total score cannot compensate for an unremarkable premise or weak use case.

**Output:** Ranked candidate field with reasons, uncertainties, and disqualifications.

### 8. Select the first asset

Choose one candidate based on:

- strongest decision value
- clearest user moment
- strongest remarkable idea
- lowest-cost credible test
- strongest learning potential
- greatest opportunity to reveal demand for adjacent assets

**Output:** **Recommended First Asset** decision brief.

## Phase 3: Design the asset experience

### 9. Write the asset promise

Complete this statement:

> When **[specific user]** encounters **[specific situation]**, this asset helps them **[perform a useful action]** so they can **[obtain a meaningful result]**.

**Output:** One clear promise.

### 10. Design the user journey

Define:

1. What attracts attention?
2. What does the user immediately recognize?
3. What do they do?
4. What do they discover?
5. What result do they receive?
6. What motivates them to save it?
7. What motivates them to share it?
8. What would bring them back?

**Output:** End-to-end asset experience.

### 11. Design the remarkable moment

Identify the specific moment that makes the asset worth talking about.

For **Same Data, Four Answers**, that might be:

> The user watches four mathematically correct reports recommend four different decisions from the same raw data.

**Output:** One deliberate Purple Cow moment, not decorative novelty.

### 12. Establish evidence and claim boundaries

Separate:

- documented facts
- Venkat’s direct experience
- supported inference
- working hypothesis
- illustrative scenarios
- unknowns
- public-safe versus restricted material

**Output:** Evidence and claims register.

### 13. Choose the smallest effective format

Determine whether the first version should be:

- a visual comparison
- a worksheet
- an interactive simulation
- a facilitated challenge
- a calculator
- a diagnostic
- a downloadable template

The format follows the required experience.

**Output:** Format recommendation and low-fidelity design.

## Phase 4: Prototype and test

### 14. Build the smallest credible prototype

Include only what is required to experience the central value.

For example:

- one dataset
- four reporting interpretations
- four resulting recommendations
- explanation of the differences
- one action the user can take afterward

**Output:** Testable prototype.

### 15. Conduct comprehension testing

Determine whether users can explain:

- what happened
- why it matters
- what they learned
- when they would use it
- what they would do next

**Output:** Comprehension evidence and confusion points.

### 16. Test actual asset behavior

Observe more than stated enthusiasm:

- Do they complete it?
- Do they save it?
- Do they download it?
- Do they retrieve it later?
- Do they apply it?
- Do they share it?
- Who do they share it with?
- What do they say when sharing it?
- Do they request another version?

**Output:** Behavioral evidence mapped to the Asset-Use Fit ladder.

### 17. Revise, reposition, or stop

Use the evidence to decide:

- continue
- simplify
- change the format
- change the audience
- change the promise
- combine with another candidate
- stop development

**Output:** Evidence-based continuation decision.

### 18. Apply the final threshold

Before release, test the completed asset again for:

- usefulness
- clarity
- ease of use
- responsible claims
- remarkability
- save motivation
- download motivation
- share motivation
- repeat-use potential
- defensibility
- economic connection

The minimum gate tests the idea. The final gate tests the actual experience.

**Output:** Release, revise, or reject decision.

- Where does it lead?
- Does it preserve the asset’s meaning?

**Output:** Purpose and fit test for every content expression.

Translate the same governed asset into channel-appropriate experiences while preserving:

- the core insight
- evidence boundaries
- definitions
- intended result
- recognizable language

**Output:** Channel-specific projections connected to one canonical asset.

- diagnosing the cause
- quantifying the cost

- testing reproducibility
- persuading leadership

Relationship is not sufficient. Each asset must create independent value.

The family as a system must demonstrate:

- a shared audience or connected user journey
- a coherent problem progression
- meaningful movement from awareness to action
- less effort or greater value through connection
- no unnecessary duplication
- consistent definitions and evidence
- a stronger reason to return
- a plausible product or commercial path

**Output:** Asset-family fit verdict.

### 25. Apply Purple Cow to the entire family

Ask:

> Is the connected experience itself worth talking about, or is it merely a collection of competent resources?

The family should produce a recognizable transformation that no individual component can create alone.

**Output:** Family-level remarkability verdict.

### 26. Identify the earned product opportunity

Repeated use may support:

- a facilitated workshop
- an organizational assessment
- a paid implementation kit
- a reporting-governance product
- an interactive software experience
- an advisory diagnostic
- an ongoing benchmarking system

Productization follows observed behavior, not asset volume.

### 27. Connect the asset to economic value

Determine whether the asset:

- creates qualified conversations
- reduces diagnostic effort
- supports a paid engagement
- becomes a paid tool
- generates proprietary learning
- strengthens proof and positioning
- creates return use or subscription potential

**Output:** Responsible value and revenue path.

### 28. Maintain provenance and maturity

Track every asset through:

**Candidate → Developed → Applied → Supported**

Publication, downloads, or engagement do not automatically advance maturity. Advancement requires evidence from relevant use.

Capture:

- who used it
- why they used it
- what changed
- where it failed
- what they requested next
- which assumptions were wrong
- which adjacent problem became visible

  → improved asset

The difference isn’t primarily visual quality. It’s how much of the user’s real problem the asset takes responsibility for solving.

| | **Simple embedded asset** | **Strong standalone prototype** | **Complex applied experience** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Solve one small job inside a larger experience | Deliver a complete insight or decision experience by itself | Help someone perform consequential work in their real environment |
| **User input** | A few answers or selections | Usually synthetic or bounded inputs | Real organizational data, definitions, documents, or decisions |
| **Output** | Checklist, card, score, map, or prompt | Complete reveal, diagnosis, or simulation | Customized analysis, operational artifact, or implemented working method |
| **Context needed** | Supplied by the parent experience | Contained within the prototype | Must be gathered from the user or organization |
| **Risk** | Low | Moderate and controllable with synthetic scenarios | Higher because accuracy, privacy, authority, and adoption matter |
| **Validation question** | “Does this component help?” | “Does the experience create the intended insight?” | “Does this improve real work and produce a better decision?” |
| **Typical build time** | 15–30 minutes | 30–90 minutes | 1.5–4 hours for a prototype, potentially days for responsible application |

## Simple embedded asset

An embedded asset performs one focused function but doesn’t create a complete experience on its own.

Examples:

- **Metric Meaning Test**
- **Analytics Ownership Map**
- **Decision Reconciliation Card**

The Decision Reconciliation Card inside *Same Data, Four Answers* is a good example. It becomes valuable after the user experiences the four conflicting answers. By itself, it would look like another reporting template.

Its job is usually to help the user:

- capture something;
- compare something;
- remember something;
- make the next step explicit.

It can be highly useful and saveable without being independently remarkable.

## Strong standalone prototype

A standalone prototype has a complete experiential arc:

```text
Promise → participation → surprising reveal → meaning → useful output
```

*Same Data, Four Answers* does this:

1. It presents a recognizable decision.
2. The user chooses an answer.
3. It reveals that every answer is mathematically correct.
4. It shows how meaning changes the decision.
5. It produces a reconciliation artifact.

It doesn’t require a company’s actual data or systems. The synthetic scenario is enough to create the central insight.

Other candidates suited to this form include:

- **Can You Reconstruct This Customer?**
- **Trust Before Tools Scorecard**
- **The Cost of One Wrong Conversion**, once its assumptions are responsible

The primary test is whether the experience is understandable, remarkable, useful, and worth saving or sharing.

## Complex applied experience

An applied experience crosses from demonstration into real work.

For example, a real **Report Reproducibility Challenge** would require:

- an actual report;
- source data;
- metric definitions;
- transformation rules;
- documentation;
- a second person or team attempting reproduction;
- comparison and discrepancy analysis;
- ownership of the resulting corrections.

Likewise, a **Tagging Translation Kit** applied to a company would need to reconcile real event names, brand conventions, platforms, business definitions, and governance authority.

The output could directly affect reporting standards, media decisions, platform configuration, or operational responsibilities. That creates greater value, but also greater evidence, privacy, accuracy, and implementation obligations.

## How they fit together

One standalone experience can contain several embedded assets and lead to a complex applied experience:

```text
Same Data, Four Answers
        ↓
Metric Meaning Test
        ↓
Decision Reconciliation Card
        ↓
Report Reproducibility Challenge
        ↓
Conversion Definition Diff
        ↓
Tagging Translation Kit
```

The first asset creates recognition. Embedded assets help the user think and retain the lesson. Applied experiences help the organization change real work.

So these aren’t three quality levels. They are three levels of responsibility:

- **Embedded:** helps with one part.
- **Standalone:** creates a complete experience.
- **Applied:** takes responsibility for improving real work.
