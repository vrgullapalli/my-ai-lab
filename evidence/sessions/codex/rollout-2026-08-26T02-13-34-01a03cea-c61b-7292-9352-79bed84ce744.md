The first thing is a clear commission. The last thing is verified closure.

A prompt alone is not a workflow. A dependable workflow needs these components:

1. **Commission**
   - What outcome is wanted?
   - Who owns it?
   - What triggers the workflow?
   - What does “done” mean?

2. **Authority boundary**
   - What may the agent read, decide, change, or send?
   - What is prohibited?
   - Which decisions remain human-only?
   - What requires explicit approval?

3. **Inputs and context**
   - Required sources and current state.
   - Which source wins when information conflicts.
   - How facts, inferences, assumptions, and Unknowns are treated.

4. **Process**
   - The ordered steps.
   - Dependencies between steps.
   - State changes and intermediate outputs.
   - Conditions for continuing, branching, or stopping.

5. **Capabilities**
   - Available tools, skills, permissions, and systems.
   - What each capability is permitted to do.
   - What happens when a required capability is unavailable.

6. **Decision and approval gates**
   - Routine decisions the agent can make.
   - Consequential decisions that return to the human.
   - Evidence the human receives at each checkpoint.

7. **Quality and acceptance**
   - Observable acceptance criteria.
   - Hard requirements that cannot be averaged away.
   - Fresh evidence showing each criterion passed.
   - The executing agent’s own “PASS” should never be sufficient by itself.

8. **Failure handling**
   - What counts as a correctable failure versus a hard stop.
   - Correction instructions.
   - Retry or time limit.
   - Escalation path when the limit is reached.

9. **Output and handoff**
   - What artifact or result is produced.
   - Where it belongs.
   - Who consumes it next.
   - Provenance, assumptions, unresolved issues, and decisions needed.

10. **Verification and closure**
    - Verify the actual result against the original definition of done.
    - Record completed, deferred, blocked, and failed items.
    - Produce a receipt or durable record.
    - Return control to the appropriate owner and stop.

The complete flow is:

**Commission → authority → context → process → execution → gates → evaluation → correction if needed → independent verification → receipt and stop**

The most important systems lesson is that **the workflow controls the agent; the agent should not control the workflow**. The model provides judgment and execution capability, but the surrounding contract supplies authority, evidence standards, stopping conditions, and accountability.

So, in direct terms:

- **Start:** “Here is the intended outcome, definition of done, and your authority.”
- **Finish:** “Here is fresh evidence that the acceptance criteria were met, the durable receipt, any remaining Unknowns, and who owns the next decision.”

Your smallest useful next step would be to turn these ten components into one reusable **Agent Workflow Contract** that every Codex, Claude, assistant, skill, or agent receives before beginning consequential work.

**Codex** · `msg_0aff829f120a4297016a8e930ddeb087d08298738ab4899d5b`

Got it—workflow.

To identify a workflow hidden inside a creator’s article, video, course, conversation, or broader intellectual assets, I would use a structured extraction and validation process. The result should not simply be “this sounds like a workflow.” It should show exactly what is present, what was inferred, what is missing, and how mature the workflow is.

## The three questions to answer

1. **Is a workflow actually present?**
2. **Is enough of it present to reconstruct and execute it?**
3. **Is it genuinely distinctive—or just a familiar process expressed differently?**

Those require different evidence.

## 1. Define the evidence boundary

First determine what is being analyzed:

- One article.
- One content series.
- One product or course.
- Everything available from the creator.
- A specific period or version of their work.

Then inventory the sources:

- Primary creator material.
- Examples and demonstrations.
- Templates, workbooks, prompts, or tools.
- Interviews and explanations.
- Corrections, objections, and negative examples.
- Derivative summaries written by others.
- Duplicates, revisions, and unavailable material.

If only one article was analyzed, the valid conclusion is:

> “This article contains a candidate workflow.”

It would not yet support:

> “This is the creator’s complete or unique workflow.”

## 2. Find workflow evidence

I would look for the following components:

| Component | Question |
|---|---|
| Job | What problem is this workflow solving? |
| Outcome | What should be different when it finishes? |
| Trigger | When should someone use it? |
| Inputs | What information, materials, or conditions are required? |
| Operator | Who performs it? |
| Authority | What may they decide, and what stays with someone else? |
| Steps | What actions happen? |
| Sequence | In what order, and which steps can repeat? |
| Decision rules | How does someone choose between different paths? |
| State | What must be remembered between steps? |
| Tools | What tools, templates, or capabilities are required? |
| Quality bar | How is good work distinguished from weak work? |
| Exceptions | What happens when the normal path does not fit? |
| Failure handling | What happens when a step fails? |
| Output | What concrete artifact or result survives? |
| Handoff | Who or what receives the output? |
| Stop rule | How does the operator know to stop? |
| Learning loop | How do results improve the next run? |

Strong evidence often appears in:

- “First I…then I…”
- “If this happens, I…”
- “I never do X until Y…”
- “Most people do X, but I do Y because…”
- Real examples.
- Corrections and refusals.
- Repeated choices across different situations.
- Templates, checklists, and intermediate artifacts.

The most valuable evidence is usually not the creator’s polished explanation. It is their repeated behavior, decisions, corrections, and exceptions.

## 3. Reconstruct it without filling the gaps

Every proposed component should connect to an exact source passage.

Each item should be labeled:

- **Directly stated**
- **Demonstrated**
- **Repeated across sources**
- **Supported inference**
- **Hypothesis**
- **Missing**
- **Contradicted**

For example:

> **Candidate decision rule:** Start with diagnosis before generating solutions.  
> **Evidence:** Demonstrated in Sources 2, 5, and 8. Explicitly stated in Source 11.  
> **Confidence:** High.  
> **Unknown:** The creator does not explain when diagnosis is sufficient to proceed.

This prevents the analyst from turning scattered observations into a process the creator never actually used.

## 4. Determine what kind of thing it is

Not every useful idea is a workflow.

- **Insight:** A useful observation.
- **Principle:** A general rule for making judgments.
- **Framework:** A way of organizing or understanding something.
- **Method:** A recognizable approach to producing a result.
- **Workflow:** A repeatable sequence that turns inputs into an outcome.
- **SOP:** Detailed instructions for performing a recurring task.
- **System:** Multiple workflows, roles, state, tools, controls, and feedback loops.
- **Intellectual asset:** Transferable judgment or method preserved in a reusable form.

A workflow requires more than a collection of principles. At minimum, it needs:

> **Trigger → inputs → ordered actions → decisions → output → stop condition**

## 5. Test whether it is complete

“Complete” depends on the intended use. I would assign one of these maturity levels:

1. **Workflow fragment**
   - A few related steps or principles are present.

2. **Candidate workflow**
   - A likely sequence can be reconstructed, but important parts are inferred or missing.

3. **Specified workflow**
   - The job, inputs, steps, decisions, outputs, quality bar, and stopping rule are documented.

4. **Executable workflow**
   - A person unfamiliar with the creator can perform it without inventing consequential details.

5. **Validated workflow**
   - It has been used on representative cases, including an exception or failure case, with observable results.

6. **Distinctive intellectual asset**
   - The workflow contains transferable judgment or a meaningful mechanism that is not merely common practice relabeled.

A workflow should not be called complete unless:

- Every source in the declared boundary was processed or reported unavailable.
- Every material workflow component has evidence or is explicitly marked missing.
- No critical contradiction remains unresolved.
- An independent person can follow it.
- The workflow handles at least one normal case and one difficult or exception case.
- The output can be evaluated against an observable quality bar.
- The process has a clear stopping condition.
- The provenance and creator attribution are preserved.

I would not use a simple percentage score. A workflow that scores 90% but lacks a decision rule or stopping condition may still be unusable.

## 6. Test whether it is truly distinctive

Uniqueness is a stronger claim than completeness.

A single article cannot prove that a workflow is unique. You would need to test:

- Does the creator apply it repeatedly across independent situations?
- Which elements are common practice?
- Which elements come from known external frameworks?
- What combination, sequence, decision rule, or quality judgment appears distinctive?
- Is the difference operationally meaningful, or merely different language?
- Does the creator’s workflow produce a recognizable result?
- Has it been compared with credible alternatives?

The right conclusion may be:

- **Common workflow, clearly explained.**
- **Known methods combined in a distinctive way.**
- **Distinctive decision logic inside an otherwise familiar workflow.**
- **Potentially original workflow; external comparison incomplete.**
- **Uniqueness unsupported.**

Naming a workflow does not make it unique. Packaging it attractively does not make it an intellectual asset. Its distinctiveness usually lives in the creator’s hidden judgment: what they notice, reject, prioritize, sequence, and use as the quality threshold.

## The final deliverable

The analysis should produce a **Workflow Evidence Card** containing:

- Workflow name—candidate unless creator-approved.
- Problem and intended outcome.
- Trigger and inputs.
- Roles and authority.
- Steps and sequence.
- Decision and branching rules.
- Required state and tools.
- Outputs and handoffs.
- Quality and acceptance criteria.
- Exceptions, failure handling, and stop rule.
- Feedback and learning loop.
- Source anchors for every claim.
- Inferences, contradictions, and Unknowns.
- Maturity verdict.
- Distinctiveness verdict.
- Rights and attribution considerations.
- The smallest validation test.

The first step is therefore **defining the source boundary and the claim you are trying to establish**.

The last step is **having someone other than the extractor run the reconstructed workflow on real cases, then recording what worked, what required guessing, and what remains unproven**.

That is how you avoid merely discovering an interesting pattern and prematurely calling it a complete, unique workflow.
