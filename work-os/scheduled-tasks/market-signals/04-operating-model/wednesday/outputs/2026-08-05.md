## 1. Business teams are building the agents, so they become the default owners

**What appears to be changing**
- Last Friday's belief: when no one decides, the team that deployed the agent owns it by default.
- This week's picture: the builder is often a business user in sales, customer success, or operations. They can switch agents on with wide access. No one reviews what the agent may do before it acts.
- So the owner by default is not even a technical team. It is whoever in the business had the idea. Inferred from the evidence below.

**Evidence so far**
- [Opsin Labs report, via Yahoo Finance, August 5](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/opsin-labs-report-60-enterprise-162100301.html). The data comes from Opsin's analysis of "enterprise production environments across eight verticals between March 2025 and June 2026." That is measured activity, not self-reported answers. Findings, observed by the vendor:
  - "67% of agents are being built by employees without engineering background like GTM, Customer Success, and Operations." GTM means go-to-market, meaning sales and marketing.
  - "Enterprise environments now average one agent, live or in draft mode, for every employee."
  - "60% of agents provisioned beyond default settings were granted allow-all access rather than being scoped to the permissions their tasks required."
  - Agents run with "limited to no human checkpoints before they act."
- [SAP News Center, August 3](https://news.sap.com/2026/08/agent-sprawl-why-ai-governance-is-now-board-level-issue/), by Paul Taylor, citing the SAP LeanIX Agentic AI Survey 2026: "less than half of the organizations surveyed have visibility into an inventory of AI agents." The piece says agent governance now "touches board-level concerns: risk ownership, regulatory exposure, data protection, auditability, operational resilience, and accountability for autonomous decisions." Vendor's own blog. Sample size not given.

**Unit of analysis**
Agents inside the companies Opsin monitors, across eight industries. I do not know if any are pharma. SAP's claim is about companies in its own survey. Any read-across to pharma commercial teams is my inference.

**Hypothesis affected**
"The pieces have owners. The connections often do not." Also the audience condition: the leader "is accountable for the outcome but is not the person implementing the technology." Here the builder is a business user, often in the leader's own team, but without a record or a review step. Territory: T06 Human-Governed Agentic Operating Systems. Secondary dependency: Trust and Control, since most agents carry more access than their job needs.

**Direction:** STRENGTHENS. It also sharpens it. The gap is not only "no named owner." It is that building an agent now takes no handoff at all, so there is no point where ownership gets assigned.

**Evidence strength:** MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR. It is the first evidence in this series drawn from live systems rather than survey answers. But it comes from a vendor that sells the fix, and the method is only described in a press release.

**Is it a pattern?**
- Yes, repeating for a third week. Six survey sponsors across the last two weeks, plus now Opsin and SAP, point the same way.
- What is new: the evidence type (measured systems) and who builds the agents (business teams).
- Duplicates: none this week. SAP repeats a Gartner figure secondhand, so I did not count it.
- Counterevidence: Opsin does not say how many agents do anything that matters. "Live or in draft mode" means many may be idle. Nothing shows a named owner failing to act.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: new tools make building easy, and rules always lag. This happened with spreadsheets and shadow software before.
- What else could explain it: this may be mostly an access-control problem. If permissions were scoped well, who built the agent would matter less. That would make it Trust and Control first.
- What would make us wrong: a company, ideally pharma, showing that business-built agents must get a named owner and a scope review before they go live, and that it works.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- A pharma or life sciences source saying whether commercial or medical staff build their own agents, and who approves them.
- Any company publishing a rule that no agent goes live without a named business owner and a scope check.
- A second measured-data source, not a survey, on who builds agents.

**State:** WAIT FOR FRIDAY. The pattern is steady and now has better evidence, but still no pharma source.

## 2. Pfizer names AI in its commercial field force, and does not say who runs it

**What appears to be changing**
- Last week, GSK and Bristol Myers Squibb tied AI to company-wide savings or "rewiring." Neither pointed at commercial work.
- This week, Pfizer tied AI to its field force and marketing, and to its cost program across "commercial, R&D and enabling functions" (R&D means research and development). That is his buyer's own function.
- Still no word on who owns the AI-enabled commercial work, who can change it, or who answers if it goes wrong.

**Evidence so far**
- [Pfizer call transcript, Investing.com, August 4](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-pfizer-beats-q2-2026-forecasts-and-lifts-revenue-outlook-93CH-4835365). Chief executive Albert Bourla: "It's helping to make our commercial field force more effective and sharpening our commercial marketing approach." Also: "Our ambition is to build an AI-native R&D organization," meaning research and development. Cecile Guegan, listed as interim chief financial officer: "Progress leveraging data and scaling AI across the company supported our field force in driving access and increasing uptake for new launches." Observed, company's own words.
- [Pfizer results release, filed with the United States Securities and Exchange Commission, August 4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000078003/000007800326000094/pfe-6282026xex99.htm). It points to "further productivity enhancements from technology and simplification efforts across our commercial, R&D and enabling functions," with R&D meaning research and development. Its risk list warns "that anticipated cost savings from AI, automation and digital enablement efforts may not be realized." Observed.
- [BioPharma Dive, August 4](https://www.biopharmadive.com/news/pfizer-cost-cuts-savings-2q-2026-earnings/826930/): Pfizer now targets $6.7 billion in total savings from its cost program. The article does not mention AI or who runs the program. Reported.
- Counterevidence, same week: [Merck and Company's results slides, August 4](https://www.merck.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/124/2026/08/2Q26-Merck-Earnings-Presentation.pdf), [Amgen's results release, August 4](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/amgen-reports-second-quarter-2026-financial-results-302842890.html), and [Novo Nordisk's results release, in a Securities and Exchange Commission filing dated August 4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000353278/000035327826000023/caq22026.htm) say nothing about AI in operations. I searched the text of each.

**Unit of analysis**
One large drugmaker's statements to investors, including its commercial field force. Not a described workflow. Not an owner.

**Hypothesis affected**
"AI deployment advances faster than the organization can define value, redesign the workflow, and prove the result." Also the audience situation: the commercial leader may meet AI as a savings target already set above them. Territory: T01 Decision Integrity Under Complexity. Secondary dependency: Use, since the claim is "more effective" with no measure given.

**Direction:** COMPLICATES. Last Friday's trigger was a third drugmaker joining. That happened, and it reached commercial work. But three of the four companies reporting this week stayed silent, and Pfizer described results, not who runs the redesigned work.

**Evidence strength:** LOW. EARLY INDICATOR. A few lines on an investor call. Pfizer has talked about AI publicly before, so this may be a repeated message, not a new change.

**Is it a pattern?** Emerging. Three of eight large drugmakers across two weeks named AI in how they operate. None named an owner. That is a pattern in what companies say, not yet in how they are organized.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: investor calls reward AI talk. "Field force more effective" can describe a better targeting list, not a new way of working.
- What else could explain it: Pfizer may have clear owners and simply not disclose them. Silence about owners is not proof of no owners.
- What would make us wrong: Pfizer or another drugmaker naming who owns AI in commercial work, with authority to change or stop it.
- Background, not a signal: the European Union's rules for high-risk AI, which include human oversight duties, were due August 2. They were pushed back. [Pinsent Masons, July 6](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/law-delaying-eu-high-risk-ai-rules-finalised): stand-alone high-risk systems now apply "from 2 December 2027." So for now, no legal clock is forcing pharma to name who oversees these systems. Inferred.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- A drugmaker still due to report in early August naming AI in commercial work, with or without an owner.
- Any Pfizer statement, interview, or job posting naming who owns AI in the field force.
- A pharma commercial leader, anywhere, describing who reviews AI suggestions before a representative acts on them.

**State:** WAIT FOR FRIDAY. More results are due this week, and they will show whether this spreads.
