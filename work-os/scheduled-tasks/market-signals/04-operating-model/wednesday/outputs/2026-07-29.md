## 1. When no one owns the agent, the team that deployed it does by default

**What appears to be changing**
- Last week the gap was "no one is named." This week's surveys show what fills the gap: the deploying team, informally, with no agreement that this is right.
- People also disagree about who *should* own it. That is a decision-rights problem, not just a staffing gap.

**Evidence so far**
- [Okta Global CISO Insights 2026, July 29](https://www.okta.com/newsroom/articles/global-ciso-insights-2026/), 306 chief information security officers and senior security leaders in six countries: "20% leave agent management to the team that deployed them on an ad hoc basis." Observed, self-reported.
- Same report: "Only 31% of CISOs feel fully aligned with their C-suite and board on acceptable AI risk." CISO means chief information security officer. In the United States the figure was 12%. Observed, self-reported.
- Same report: "46% can control what agents access" and "45% can authorize what agents do." Observed, self-reported.
- [1Password survey, covered by Help Net Security, July 29](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/07/29/1password-ai-agent-governance/), 1,000 security and engineering staff at large United States firms, fielded late May to early June: "Nobody agrees who answers for an agent's actions." About 5% said the agent itself is accountable. Observed, self-reported.
- [Kiteworks, July 29](https://www.kiteworks.com/cybersecurity-risk-management/ai-agents-governance-gap-surveys/), summarizing the same 1Password data: "65% of respondents believe a different person should be accountable when an agent causes harm than currently is." This is a vendor recap of the two surveys above, so it counts once, not twice.
- [Tines launch, SiliconANGLE, July 28](https://siliconangle.com/2026/07/28/tines-launches-3b-help-enterprises-govern-ai-built-apps-agents/): chief executive Eoin Hinchy says the hard part is "knowing what's running, whether you can trust it, and who's responsible for it." The article cites an IBM survey from June: "77% of the 2,000 technology executives it surveyed across 33 geographies said AI adoption at their organizations had already outpaced governance capabilities." A vendor claim plus an older survey, context only.

**Unit of analysis**
Security and engineering leaders at large companies, mostly outside the United States in Okta's case. Not pharma. Not commercial teams. Any read-across to pharma is my inference.

**Hypothesis affected**
"The pieces have owners. The connections often do not." Also the problem hypothesis: "The accountable leader receives the uncertainty that upstream teams and systems did not resolve." The Okta alignment gap shows the uncertainty now sits between security and the executive team, not just inside one function. Territory: T06 Human-Governed Agentic Operating Systems. Secondary dependency: Trust and Control, since fewer than half can see or limit what agents do.

**Direction:** STRENGTHENS.

**Evidence strength:** MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR. Two new independent surveys, a new group of respondents, the same direction as last week's three. Still all self-reported, and every sponsor sells a fix.

**Is it a pattern?**
- Yes, repeating. Five independent surveys across two weeks now point the same way: Avalara, Booz Allen, BCG, 1Password, and Okta.
- What is new: the "deploying team by default" answer. That is a clearer picture of how ownership forms when no one decides it.
- Duplicates: Kiteworks repeats 1Password and Okta. Tines leans on June IBM data.
- Counterevidence: most respondents still say something is in place. Okta's 20% is a minority. The surveys do not test whether named owners actually act.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: agents are new, and security teams always feel less aligned with leadership than other functions do.
- What else could explain it: this could be mostly a visibility problem. If fewer than half can see all their agents, the ownership question may be secondary. That would make it Trust and Control first, Operating Model second.
- What would make us wrong: a pharma source showing agents in commercial work already have a named owner through medical, legal, and regulatory review.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- A pharma or life sciences survey that asks who is accountable for an agent's error.
- Any company, pharma or not, publishing a rule that the person who grants an agent's access owns its actions.
- A governance tool that will not let an agent go live without a named owner.

**State:** WAIT FOR FRIDAY. The pattern is steady, not speeding up, and it still has no pharma evidence.

## 2. Pharma commercial agents are being run by the vendor

**What appears to be changing**
- Last week this was a finance survey line and one vendor's product pitch. This week a large pharma data vendor reports hundreds of live commercial agents across nearly all top pharma companies.
- If the vendor builds and runs the agents, the pharma company may own the outcome while the vendor holds the know-how.

**Evidence so far**
- [IQVIA call summary, BigGo Finance, July 28](https://finance.biggo.com/news/US_IQV_2026-07-28): "AI traction accelerated in commercial, with 294 agents deployed across 90 use cases and 19 of the top 20 pharma companies now using IQVIA's AI solutions." Claimed by the vendor, reported secondhand.
- Same summary: one mid-size pharma client is expanding IQVIA AI "across an immunology franchise in 95 nations." The piece does not say who runs the agents day to day.
- [IQVIA's own July 28 results release](https://ir.iqvia.com/press-releases/press-release-details/2026/IQVIA-Reports-Second-Quarter-2026-Results/default.aspx) says only that "increased adoption of our AI solutions" helped growth. The 294 figure is not in the release. I could only confirm it through the call summary.

**Unit of analysis**
One vendor's reported deployments inside pharma commercial teams. The count is the vendor's. "Using IQVIA's AI solutions" may mean anything from one dashboard to a live agent.

**Hypothesis affected**
The need hypothesis: "Agencies may need a credible client-facing capability without hiring a complete executive function or buying another disconnected tool." Also the audience condition: the leader "is accountable for the outcome but is not the person implementing the technology." Secondary dependency: Repeatability and Scale, since 90 use cases across many clients suggests the vendor is the one standardizing.

**Direction:** COMPLICATES. The gap in in-house know-how is real in pharma. But the vendor is already filling it inside his buyers' commercial teams.

**Evidence strength:** LOW. EARLY INDICATOR. One vendor, secondhand, promotional. But it is the first pharma-specific count.

**Is it a pattern?** Repeating as a theme, now with pharma evidence. Still one source per week, so not yet a strong pattern.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: an earnings call is where a vendor puts its best number.
- What else could explain it: pharma companies may run these agents themselves on the vendor's platform, the way they run customer relationship software. Then this is a Foundation story, not an ownership one.
- What would make us wrong: a pharma company describing its own team that monitors, changes, and can switch off vendor agents.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- Any IQVIA client, or any agency, saying who monitors these agents and who can stop them.
- A pharma job posting that owns vendor agents after launch, with stop or rollback authority.

**State:** WAIT FOR FRIDAY. Early and vendor-sourced. Nothing is lost by waiting.

## 3. GSK is counting on AI to redesign support work, and has not said who runs it

**What appears to be changing**
- A large drugmaker is booking savings against AI-enabled process redesign before saying how the redesigned work will be owned.

**Evidence so far**
- [GSK results notice, July 28](https://www.investegate.co.uk/announcement/rns/gsk--gsk/2nd-quarter-results/9691506): "Cost savings are expected to be enabled by technology and AI and generated by streamlining support services and process redesign including procurement delivery." The target is £1.9 billion a year by 2029. Observed, the company's own words.
- [Investing.com on the results slides, July 28](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/gsk-q2-2026-slides-specialty-growth-drives-beat-20-phase-3-starts-93CH-4817878): about "45% from streamlining support services and enhanced procurement." Reported.
- [STAT, July 28](https://www.statnews.com/2026/07/28/gsk-earnings-report-luke-miels-strategy-late-stage-clinical-trials/): "Miels would not say Tuesday how many jobs would be cut under the savings drive." Luke Miels is GSK's chief executive.

**Unit of analysis**
One pharma company's support functions and procurement. Not commercial. Not customer data.

**Hypothesis affected**
"AI deployment advances faster than the organization can define value, redesign the workflow, and prove the result." Territory: T01 Decision Integrity Under Complexity. Secondary dependency: Use, since the savings target is set before the workflow is described.

**Direction:** TOO EARLY TO TELL.

**Evidence strength:** LOW. EARLY INDICATOR. One sentence in a results notice. Cost programs often name technology as the lever.

**Is it a pattern?** Interesting signal. No pattern yet.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: standard cost-cutting language. "Technology and AI" may be a label, not a plan.
- What else could explain it: GSK may have owners in place and simply not publish them. Absence of disclosure is not absence of ownership.
- What would make us wrong: GSK naming a transformation owner with authority over the redesigned processes.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- Another large drugmaker in this week's results naming AI as a source of savings. That would make it a pattern.
- GSK describing who owns the redesigned support work, or which functions move to shared services.

**State:** WAIT FOR FRIDAY. One company, one line. Other drugmakers' results this summer will show whether it spreads.
