## The short answer

It was a thin week for Repeatability and Scale. Two early signals are worth holding. Neither is a pattern yet, and neither needs action before Friday.

1. When pharma AI travels, the part that travels seems to be a method and a body of data, not a tool. Who gets to reuse that data is now being fought over in deals.
2. Putting AI live is not the same as keeping it live. One claim this week says most live customer-facing AI agents get rolled back, and experience does not fix it.

A note on what was not there: no pharma company this week showed a commercial AI capability moving from one brand, market, or team to another. That absence is itself worth tracking.

## Pattern 1: The reusable part is a method and a data asset, and reuse rights are now contested

**What appears to be changing**
- The unit that moves across programs is a way of working plus the data behind it. Deal makers now treat the right to reuse data as a main term, not fine print.

**Evidence so far**
- Bristol Myers Squibb expanded its Nvidia work to build what it calls an AI factory. Its "Predict First" method "now informs every small molecule program and most large molecule programs." AI handles "complex, data-intensive tasks while scientists focus on direction, interpretation and decisions" ([PharmExec, July 20](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/bristol-myers-squibb-collaboration-nvidia--ai-)). The same outlet's daily summary says the setup supports research "across oncology, hematology, cardiovascular disease, immunology, and neuroscience" ([PharmExec Daily, July 20](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/daily-bristol-myers-squibb--partnership-nvidia)). That is the same event, not a second source. The method's spread is claimed by the company. No outcome data was given.
- BioSpace reports that data reuse is now a sticking point in AI drug deals. "Previously, biotech platform companies would hand off most of the data generated during a licensing deal, keeping partnership programs siloed." That has "changed as AI companies need that information." Xaira's Rachel Lane: "Those types of deals are not going to fly these days." Bayer's Deepa Talpade: "Pharmas have long wanted to have strict control over the data developed in a partnership" ([BioSpace, July 22](https://www.biospace.com/business/ai-data-becomes-sticking-point-in-biopharma-dealmaking)). Observed shift in deal talks, reported through named people.

**Unit of analysis**
- Research-side AI methods at one large drugmaker, and data terms in AI drug-discovery partnerships. This is not yet a claim about commercial or customer-data work.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Territory T05, Transferable Operating Intelligence: which parts of expert judgment can be made reusable. Secondary dependency: Foundation (who owns the data).
- Direction: **COMPLICATES.** It supports the idea that a method can travel across programs. But "most" large molecule programs, not all, shows the portable unit has edges. And the fight over data rights says reuse depends on ownership terms, not only on technology.

**Evidence strength: LOW. EARLY INDICATOR.**
- Two independent sources, both in research, not commercial. One is a company claim with no outcome numbers.

**Pattern basis**
- The two items point at the same thing from different sides: one company reusing a method internally, and the market arguing over who may reuse data externally.
- Nothing yet shows an outcome repeating under changed conditions.
- Interesting signal. No pattern yet.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: big companies buy big compute and describe it as a factory. That is a spending story.
- What else: "informs every program" may mean the method is consulted, not that it changes results the same way everywhere.
- What would make us wrong: evidence that programs using Predict First get no better or faster than those that do not, or that data-reuse terms stay rare.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- Any number showing whether Predict First changed cycle time or success rate across more than one disease area.
- A signed deal this week where data reuse terms are disclosed.
- Any commercial-side company saying which parts of an AI capability it reuses across brands and which it rebuilds.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** Nothing here gets worse by waiting two days.

## Pattern 2: Live AI does not stay live, and experience does not seem to help

**What appears to be changing**
- The gap is not getting AI running. It is keeping people willing to act on it once it runs, in a new setting.

**Evidence so far**
- A PharmExec piece on patient support cites "one 2026 study" that "found 74% of organizations have been forced to shut down or roll back a live AI customer communications agent, a rate that holds across every region and industry and does not improve with experience or investment" ([PharmExec, July 17](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/from-dr-google-claude-important-patient-support-innovation-human-connection)). The article does not name the study. **I could not verify the source.** Treat the number as a claim. The author's own view: AI should be "equipping the person on the phone, not replacing them."
- A second PharmExec piece, by Tim Wahlberg of Chemical Abstracts Service, says adoption "drops sharply in the domains that matter most for discovery: 42% for generative design, 40% for biomarker analysis, and 29% for ADME prediction." ADME means absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion. He credits the numbers to Drug Discovery News, with no link. His read: the bottleneck is "not the models themselves, but the fragmented data environment underneath them," and scientists need "traceability, reproducibility, and defensibility" before they act ([PharmExec, July 22](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/investing-ai-just-not-where-matters-most)). The author works for a data company, so he has a stake in that answer.

**Unit of analysis**
- Customer-facing AI agents across industries (claimed), and AI use in pharma research domains (claimed). Two different units. They are linked by inference only.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Problem hypothesis: "AI deployment advances faster than the organization can define value, redesign the workflow, and prove the result." Secondary dependency: Trust and Control.
- Direction: **TOO EARLY TO TELL.** If the 74% figure is real and "does not improve with experience," that cuts against the common belief that the second deployment is easier than the first. But the source is unnamed.

**Evidence strength: LOW. EARLY INDICATOR.**
- Both numbers are secondhand, without links. One writer has a commercial interest.

**Pattern basis**
- Both items say the same thing in different settings: AI that reaches production still fails the "will people keep using it here" test.
- These are two opinion pieces in one outlet, not independent measurements.
- Interesting signal. No pattern yet.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: early AI is brittle, and rollbacks are normal growing pains.
- What else: rollback may reflect poor first builds, not a limit on portability. Low research adoption may reflect normal scientific caution.
- Check outside the lens: this may be a Trust and Control problem, or weak data (Foundation), before it is a scale problem.
- What would make us wrong: the named study turns out to measure something narrower, or rollback rates fall sharply for teams on their second or third deployment.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- The name and method of the 74% study.
- Any pharma company reporting whether a second deployment of the same AI tool held up in a new brand, market, or team.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** The key number is unverified, so acting on it now would be premature.
