## Pattern 1: Scale across units is rare, and what travels is how the portfolio is run

**What appears to be changing**
- Inferred: surveys now separate "we use AI" from "AI works in more than one part of the business." The second group is small. What sets it apart is a management habit, not a tool.

**Evidence so far**
- Observed, research firm survey, September 2: "Fewer than one-quarter of enterprises have successfully scaled AI across multiple business units." Gartner "surveyed more than 1,300 leaders from organizations with more than $50 million in revenue between January and April of this year." Even so, "85% of tech leaders" plan to raise AI spending next year. Companies that track returns, treat AI as a portfolio, and "discontinue underperforming initiatives reported positive returns in 81% of their AI initiatives." About 11% "lacked visibility into what they spent on AI in 2025" ([CIO Dive, September 2](https://www.ciodive.com/news/rising-spend-cios-AI-success/829484/)).
- Same survey, same day: the exact share is 22%. Low performers did not know the return on 29% of their projects. Gartner's Tina Nunno: "This lack of financial visibility heightens risk as spending accelerates" ([TechEdgeAI, September 2](https://techedgeai.com/gartner-survey-finds-only-22-of-organizations-have-successfully-scaled-ai-across-multiple-business-units/)). Gartner's own release would not open.
- Background, June, life sciences: of 150 executives, "45% said their AI initiatives have produced measurable improvement, including 13% who reported measurable improvements at scale" ([Deloitte, June 25](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/health-care/midyear-2026-life-sciences-outlook.html)).

**Unit of analysis**
- Leaders at companies with more than $50 million in revenue, all industries, self-reported. Not pharma. The life sciences number comes from a different survey with a different question.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Problem hypothesis: "AI deployment advances faster than the organization can define value, redesign the workflow, and prove the result." Territory: Transferable Operating Intelligence. Secondary dependency: Operating Model, because someone has to own the tracking and the stopping.
- What travels, inferred: the habit of measuring each use and cutting the weak ones. That fits last Friday's point from the Tufts study: the way of measuring travels, the number does not yet.
- Direction: **STRENGTHENS.**

**Evidence strength: MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR.**
- Large, independent, with a test of scale that fits this pillar: more than one business unit. Still self-reported. And the 81% is a link, not proof of cause.

**Pattern basis**
- Reinforces last week's McKinsey split: 40% of big firms said they were scaling agents, while the share seeing profit held flat. Gartner's stricter test lands near half that share. Different questions, same shape: breadth outruns proof.
- Life sciences sits lower in Deloitte's survey, 13% at scale. Different sample and wording, so not a direct comparison.
- Duplicates: the Gartner story ran in several outlets. Counted once.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: research firms find the gap their advice fills.
- What else could explain it: high performers may have picked easier uses first. Then the 81% reflects what they chose, not how they managed it.
- What would make us wrong: firms that track and cut projects spread no further across units than firms that do not. Or a drugmaker shows scale across brands with no such discipline.
- Outside this lens: not knowing your own spend is a Foundation and Operating Model gap. It shows up here because you cannot repeat what you never measured.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- A split of the Gartner survey by function, especially sales and marketing, or by industry.
- A pharma leader saying in public that a pilot was stopped or narrowed before it was widened.
- Lundbeck or the unnamed top 20 drugmaker naming the number its first phase moved.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** It sharpens Friday's read, but it is cross-industry and needs no move now.

**Pattern status vs. last week:** repeated and strengthened. A second large survey in two weeks, with a stricter test of scale.

## Pattern 2: The deployment work travels in people, not in the product

**What appears to be changing**
- Inferred: what makes enterprise AI work at each new customer is still done by engineers placed inside that customer. If what they learn stays with them, each rollout is repeated custom work, not reuse.

**Evidence so far**
- Observed, trade press, September 1: forward deployed engineers "embed directly inside a client's environment to develop and deploy software within that enterprise." A study by the executive search firm Christian & Timbers counts about 17,000 in the United States. Only 2,000 have "turned enterprise AI into meaningful return-on-investment." Its JC Christian: "You need to turn them into elite FDEs [forward deployed engineers] by working with them on problems at scale...Once you have that, then you're getting into a proliferation of knowledge" ([Washington Technology, September 1](https://www.washingtontechnology.com/companies/2026/09/subset-tech-talent-supply-crunch-watch-out/415572/)).
- Background, pharma, August 13: Axtria will grow its forward deployed engineering team to 1,000 by December. It cites "growing customer demand for engineers who can deploy, scale, and operationalize enterprise AI across commercial, medical, and market access functions" ([PR Newswire, August 13](http://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/axtria-to-build-industrys-largest-pharma-specific-ai-engineering-team-scaling-to-1-000-by-december-2026--302850739.html)).
- Background, August 19: "Buying AI is easier than integrating it into productive workflows" ([Dice, August 19](https://www.dice.com/career-advice/forward-deployed-engineer-shortage-is-holding-back-enterprise-ai-roi)).
- Carried from last week: Eversana sells people plus a platform. Its August 28 event write-up mentions a client panel with Lundbeck but reports no result ([PM360, August 28](https://pm360online.com/eversana-hosts-80-companies-together-with-google-cloud-to-showcase-whats-next-in-ai-agency-commercialization/)).

**Unit of analysis**
- How enterprise AI gets delivered: engineers from vendors and service firms placed with customers. A cross-industry count, plus one pharma service firm's hiring plan.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Need hypothesis: "Agencies may need a credible client-facing capability without hiring a complete executive function or buying another disconnected tool." Territory: Transferable Operating Intelligence. Secondary dependency: Operating Model.
- Direction: **COMPLICATES.** It supports last week's read that the capability is rented and held in the vendor's people. But Christian says know-how can pass from person to person. That is repeatability by apprenticeship, not by product. Unknown: whether the drugmaker ends up owning any of it.

**Evidence strength: LOW. REINFORCING INDICATOR.**
- One count from a firm that recruits these people. Hiring plans show demand, not deployments that repeat.

**Pattern basis**
- Reinforcing: Axtria's 1,000 engineers for pharma, Eversana's people-plus-platform model, and the cross-industry count all put people at the center of each rollout.
- Against: Salesforce quoted a small drugmaker that was "live in under six weeks" ([Salesforce, May 19](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/agentforce-life-sciences-140-organizations/)). Background, and a customer the vendor picked. Veeva says Falcon needs no data mapping. But its chief executive also said: "If we had our early adopters live and successful right now... Falcon would be flying off the shelf" ([TIKR, August 27](https://www.tikr.com/blog/veeva-systems-grew-revenue-18-in-q2-earnings-crm-win-backs-are-only-half-the-story)). No one is live yet.
- In pharma: **Interesting signal. No pattern yet.** One hiring plan and one vendor's model.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: new technology always needs hands-on setup at first. Later it gets packaged.
- What else could explain it: service firms earn more from people-heavy delivery, so they call it the answer. Background, February: Constellation Research's editor wrote that these engineers "are often used as a crutch to smooth over product immaturity," and "The enterprise vendors that win will take what FDEs [forward deployed engineers] learn to embed them in products" ([Constellation Research, February 1](https://www.constellationr.com/insights/news/forward-deployed-engineers-promise-peril-ai-deployments)).
- What would make us wrong: a vendor shows its second and third rollouts need far fewer engineers than the first.
- Outside this lens: part of this is Foundation. The engineers are needed because each customer's data and workflows differ.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- Axtria, Eversana, or another pharma service firm saying how much of each rollout is reused versus rebuilt.
- Engineer weeks per rollout falling from the first client to later ones.
- A Falcon go-live, with setup time and any mapping it needed.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** Low evidence and no dated event before Friday.

**Pattern status vs. last week:** "Expansion carries the vendor along" held on the buyer side, with no new drugmaker move. Its people side came back, cross-industry.
