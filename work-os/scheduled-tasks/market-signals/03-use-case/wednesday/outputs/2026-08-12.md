## Pattern 1: A seller names a field job for AI: turning rep notes into fixes

**What appears to be changing**
- In past weeks, field AI claims were broad, such as "a more effective field force." This week a seller described one job with a trigger, an output, and a human handoff.
- The job: after a doctor visit, the rep speaks notes. AI checks them for compliance and flags barriers to treatment. A person acts on the barrier.

**Evidence so far**
- Matt Farrell, Veeva's president of commercial strategy, August 10: reps "can record natural language notes with agentic voice capture while it's fresh in their mind," and "When field teams use an agentic call report, we see that 65% of compliant, free-text field notes identify actionable treatment barriers not captured by traditional reports" ([Pharmaceutical Executive, August 10](https://www.pharmexec.com/view/generating-commercial-evidence-agentic-field-brand-engagement-accelerate-launch-performance)). Claimed. No sample, method, or company named.
- Same piece, the one worked example: "one company discovered a hospital-wide economic justification requirement that was taking up to four weeks, so patients were moving to a different medicine that was not the clinical preference of their HCP. The issue was escalated to a reimbursement account manager to gain hospital-level approval." HCP means healthcare professional. Claimed. Company not named.
- Same piece: "using Commercial Evidence can accelerate launch performance by more than 10%." Claimed. No source or study cited.
- August 11: Lilly committed to Veeva's CRM system globally ([Veeva release via PR Newswire, August 11](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/veeva-vault-crm-selected-by-eli-lilly-and-company-302847385.html)). Giuseppe Firenze, a Lilly senior vice president for field engagement: "Vault CRM will help Lilly bring our breakthrough medicines to more patients." Observed as an announcement.
- The release points readers to "the Agentic Call Report to generate Commercial Evidence." It does not say Lilly will use it ([Veeva's copy, August 11](https://www.veeva.com/resources/veeva-vault-crm-selected-by-eli-lilly-and-company/)). Observed.

**Unit of analysis**
- One vendor's description of one field workflow. Plus one drugmaker's choice of CRM platform. The platform choice is not proof of the AI job.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Problem hypothesis: "AI deployment advances faster than the organization can define value, redesign the workflow, and prove the result."
- Primary lens: Use Case. Secondary: Operating Model. The value depends on who owns the barrier once it is flagged. In the example, a reimbursement account manager did.
- Direction: **COMPLICATES.** The most specific field use case in the series so far. But the proof is the seller's, and the one buyer named no AI job.

**Evidence strength: LOW. EARLY INDICATOR.**
- One seller, writing in trade press about its own product, with unsourced numbers. The Lilly deal is real, but it is a platform buy, not a use case.

**Pattern basis**
- Rhymes with Pfizer's field force claim last week. Same work area, but a buyer then and a seller now. No sign it is the same tool. Inferred.
- The article and the Lilly release come from the same seller. Not independent.
- Interesting signal. No pattern yet.

**Challenge**
- Simplest reading: a seller placing its story next to a big customer win.
- Strongest alternative: the 65% may count how often notes mention a barrier, not whether anyone fixed it or whether patients got the drug. Inferred.
- What would make us wrong: a drugmaker names this job in its own words, with a count of barriers fixed.
- Outside this lens: if barriers get flagged and nobody owns the fix, that is an ownership failure, not a bad use case.

**Watch next**
- Lilly, or any drugmaker, saying it uses AI call notes to find access barriers.
- Veeva publishing how it got the 65% or the 10%.
- A second seller describing the same job: rep notes turned into access fixes.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** One seller's claim will not change by Friday, and nothing here is time-sensitive.

## Pattern 2: Doctor-data sellers shrink AI to narrow jobs while drug company budgets stay tight

**What appears to be changing**
- Sellers of doctor data are fitting AI to small, named marketing jobs: building a doctor list in plain words, searching by conversation, showing copay savings while the doctor prescribes.
- The AI line grows while the rest shrinks. Drug company money stays tight.

**Evidence so far**
- OptimizeRx, August 12 ([release](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/12/3344034/0/en/optimizerx-reports-second-quarter-2026-financial-results-and-updates-fiscal-year-2026-guidance.html)): revenue "$20.5 million," down from "$29.2 million" a year earlier. Net revenue retention "90%," down from "121%." Top 20 drugmakers' share of revenue "54%," down from "59%." Observed.
- OptimizeRx call, same day ([Investing.com transcript, August 12](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-optimizerx-beats-q2-2026-profit-view-as-shares-slip-93CH-4856270)): "Our AI-enabled software offerings, which grew 25% year-over-year." Its audience tool "enables pharmaceutical marketers and agencies to build highly customized healthcare provider audiences using simple natural language prompts." And: "Manufacturers are still being careful with spending because of macroeconomic and regulatory uncertainty." Claimed.
- Same call, a second job: CopayCue "delivers savings information directly within the prescribing workflow at the exact moment physicians are making treatment decisions." Claimed. Clear trigger. No result given.
- Definitive Healthcare, August 10 ([Investing.com transcript, August 10](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-definitive-healthcare-tops-q2-2026-eps-forecast-93CH-4850490)): its AI platform, Turbo, starts "with an initial stage pilot with a select group of strategic customers this month," including life sciences. "We're not anticipating any material impact to the 2026 top-line revenue." "Large pharma budgets have not materially improved." Claimed.
- Same call, usage: "Multi-turn conversational search now accounts for roughly 40% of interactions with our AI search feature." Its two named examples were a medical device company and a logistics company, not drugmakers. Claimed.

**Unit of analysis**
- Two sellers of doctor data and reach to drug companies and agencies. The jobs belong to the marketer and the analyst: building audiences and researching targets. Not the doctor's work, and not the rep's.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Same problem hypothesis. Primary lens: Use Case. Secondary: Foundation. Both sellers pitch AI as a new front door to data they already own.
- Direction: **COMPLICATES.** Narrow jobs are being built, which fits the idea that narrow beats broad. But growth is counted as the seller's AI revenue or usage, and buyer budgets are not growing.

**Evidence strength: MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR**, for the narrow point only: sellers' AI lines grow while drug company spend stays tight. Three separate sellers said it in two weeks, counting Doximity last week ("The overall pharma spending environment remains tight"). Low for any claim about buyer value.

**Pattern basis**
- Repeats Doximity last week. Filed revenue figures and spoken claims agree.
- Contradicting: Definitive said "biopharma had its strongest new business quarter in three years," but did not tie those wins to AI.
- Noise: "AI-enabled" is the seller's label. Up 25% inside a business down 30% could be relabeling. Inferred.

**Challenge**
- Simplest reading: sellers under revenue pressure lead with AI.
- Strongest alternative: old jobs, list building and search, on a new screen.
- What would make us wrong: a drug company or agency says the plain-language tool changed who builds audiences, or how fast, with a before and after.

**Watch next**
- An agency naming OptimizeRx's audience tool in real use. Its June 25 launch said integration was set for August. From a search snippet, not verified at the source.
- Definitive naming a drugmaker in its Turbo pilot, or saying what the pilot measures.
- Any of these sellers reporting a buyer outcome instead of usage.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** A steady trend, not a fast one.

## Pattern 3: Nobody named a buyer measure. Every number was the seller's.

**What appears to be changing**
- Nothing, and that is the answer to Friday's question for this half week. From August 7 to 12, no buyer said what it counts as commercial AI value.

**Evidence so far**
- Veeva's "more than 10%" and "65%" (Pattern 1): no source or study. Claimed.
- Definitive: "One partner running campaigns on our audiences reported registration rates well above the benchmarks they typically expect." No partner, no number. Claimed.
- OptimizeRx: no prescribing lift or return figure, in the release or on the call. Observed.
- No analyst asked about AI on either call. Observed.

**Unit of analysis:** public statements from three sellers and one buyer. Disclosure, not practice.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Same hypothesis. Primary: Use Case. Secondary: Trust and Control. Direction: **STRENGTHENS**, narrowly. Value is defined by the seller, when at all. Territory: Decision Integrity Under Complexity.

**Evidence strength: MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR.** Fourth week, same shape. But silence in public is not proof buyers measure nothing in private.

**Pattern basis**
- Repeating. Last week: Pfizer's bundled savings, Doximity's usage, Indegene's private outcome. No second outcome-priced deal or named Indegene measure found, August 7 to 12. Nothing contradicting.

**Challenge**
- Simplest reading: nobody discloses marketing results in public. Buyers may measure privately.
- What would make us wrong: one drug company naming its measure for an AI tool, against a baseline.

**Watch next**
- A drug company or agency naming the outcome it bought from AI search, AI call notes, or AI audience building.
- Veeva or Doximity publishing the method behind one of their numbers.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** Friday's investigation stands. Nothing new to act on midweek.
