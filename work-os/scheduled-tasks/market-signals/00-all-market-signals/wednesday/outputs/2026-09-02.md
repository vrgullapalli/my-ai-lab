## Pattern 1: Outcome pricing moves "what counts as done" into the contract

**What appears to be changing**
- Inferred: sellers are starting to charge for finished work, not seats. So the definition of a finished outcome is becoming a contract term. Whoever writes that definition decides what "worked" means.

**Evidence so far**
- CIO Dive, August 31, on agent pricing. Zendesk's Chris Donato: "If the AI resolves 90% of the problem, but 10% goes to a human agent, we don't count it." A university chief information officer, Seton Hall's Paul Fisher: the model "adds complication to contract negotiation as you need to be very specific on what the desired outcomes are" ([CIO Dive, August 31](https://www.ciodive.com/news/agentic-ai-outcome-pricing-models/829023/)). Observed statements.
- Same story, the counterweight. Gartner's Tom Coshow: "Right now, what we see is that the increase in outcome-based pricing is more buzz than reality." Only 19% of services buyers and 13% of seller agreements use it so far.
- CIO Dive, September 2. Amazon Web Services is pushing its partners toward pricing that "ties costs to measurable results," with units like "per workflow executed, document processed, insight, token or solved customer case." Its own market study says "80% of customers were shifting to outcome-based commercial models" ([CIO Dive, September 2](https://www.ciodive.com/news/aws-pushes-partners-to-rethink-pricing-for-the-ai-era/829370/)). Vendor claim.
- CIO Dive, September 2, on a Gartner survey of "more than 1,300 leaders": "fewer than one-quarter of enterprises have successfully scaled AI across multiple business units," while "85% of tech leaders" plan to raise AI spending next year. "About 11%" said they "lacked visibility into what they spent on AI in 2025." Gartner's Tina Nunno: "Without disciplined measurement tied directly to business outcomes, organizations risk wasted resources" ([CIO Dive, September 2](https://www.ciodive.com/news/rising-spend-cios-AI-success/829484/)). Independent research firm.
- Background, before the window: Deloitte's April survey of 150 life sciences executives found "45% said their AI initiatives have produced measurable improvement, including 13% who reported measurable improvements at scale" ([Deloitte, June 25](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/insights/industry/health-care/midyear-2026-life-sciences-outlook.html)).

**Unit of analysis**
- How enterprise AI is priced and justified, across industries. Two sellers, one cloud vendor, one research firm, one buyer. I found no pharma commercial AI deal priced on outcomes.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Problem hypothesis: "AI deployment advances faster than the organization can define value, redesign the workflow, and prove the result." Territory: Decision Integrity Under Complexity. Pillars: Trust and Control, then Use.
- **Direction: STRENGTHENS.** Last Friday's insight was "when the buyer does not define the measure, the seller does." This week the seller's measure starts to become the invoice.

**Evidence strength: MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR.**
- For the proof gap, this is the sixth week, with an independent Gartner number added. For outcome pricing itself, it is early. Gartner says it is mostly talk.

**Pattern basis**
- Reinforcing: Gartner's "fewer than one-quarter" sits beside last week's Infosys "two-thirds" who struggle to measure return on investment and Protiviti's 35%. Different sponsors, same shape.
- Reinforcing: Zendesk counting only full resolutions and Salesforce counting its own work units last week are two sellers defining the unit.
- Against: Gartner also found that companies that "constantly track the ROI of their AI initiatives," meaning return on investment, "see greater returns." Measurement is possible when someone owns it.
- Against: 19% and 13% adoption. This may stay a pitch.

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: vendors want pricing that grows with use, and "outcome" is a friendly label for it.
- What else could explain it: outcome pricing may suit narrow tasks like ticket closing and never reach judgment work like commercial pharma.
- What would make us wrong: buyers sign outcome deals with vendor-written definitions and no disputes follow.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- A pharma commercial vendor (Veeva, Salesforce, IQVIA, Eversana) prices an AI product per outcome, such as per approved content piece or per completed call report.
- A buyer, in any industry, says in public that it rewrote a vendor's outcome definition.
- A pharma-specific survey repeats the "spend rising, scale missing" split.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** It sharpens the question already in use from last Friday ("who produced the number?"), and nothing in pharma moved this week.

## Pattern 2: Leaders trust their agents more than they check them

**What appears to be changing**
- Inferred: agents are spreading across companies faster than anyone confirms what they can reach and do. Confidence stands in for evidence. Outside testing of agents is starting to appear as a response.

**Evidence so far**
- Cequence and Enterprise Management Associates, August 31, survey of 202 technology and security leaders: "94% of enterprise IT and security leaders," meaning information technology, "are confident their AI agents do not have more access than they need, yet only 33% actually provision agents with least-privilege access." Also: "65% have experienced an AI agent take an action outside its intended scope, including 29% with measurable business impact." The research lead: "The gap is between what's written down and what's enforced when an agent takes an action nobody approved" ([GlobeNewswire, August 31](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/31/3353329/0/en/new-cequence-ema-research-94-of-enterprises-trust-their-ai-agents-aren-t-over-provisioned-only-33-actually-enforce-it.html)). Vendor-sponsored. Cequence sells agent security.
- Same study, covered September 1: "Only 32.2% of respondents said they could detect and contain an out-of-scope action within minutes using automated mechanisms" ([Infosecurity Magazine, September 1](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/65-percent-enterprises-ai-agents/)). Counted with the above, not separately.
- CIO Dive, September 2. KPMG had an internal agent tested by the Artificial Intelligence Underwriting Company against its AIUC-1 standard: "more than 900 technical tests including hallucinations, high-risk domain interactions, content safety, prompt injection attacks." KPMG's own leader: "The certification is evidence of rigor, but it doesn't actually eliminate or provide immunity from risks. Maintaining confidence will require continued monitoring, testing and human accountability" ([CIO Dive, September 2](https://www.ciodive.com/news/kpmg-agentic-tool-certified-security-reliability-ai-risks-mount/829498/)). Observed buyer act.

**Unit of analysis**
- How large companies control what their agents can reach and do, as reported by technology and security leaders. Plus one firm's single internal agent. Not pharma, and not commercial work.

**Hypothesis affected**
- Core belief: "Reliance should be proportional to evidence." And: "Trust is not added afterward... It is earned by the way the capability works." Territory: Human-Governed Agentic Operating Systems. Pillar: Trust and Control.
- **Direction: STRENGTHENS.** The survey measures the gap between a stated control and a working one. The KPMG quote says what green proves and what it does not.

**Evidence strength: LOW. EARLY INDICATOR.**
- One vendor survey whose sponsor sells the fix, and one certification that helps both the tester and the tested.

**Pattern basis**
- Reinforcing: last week Talkdesk found 52% name trust in AI decisions as a main concern. Different question, same direction.
- Reinforcing: last Friday's open question, "who checks the AI summary before it counts as evidence," is the same gap, one level down.
- Against: KPMG reported "No critical or major vulnerabilities were identified." A well-built agent can pass an outside test.
- Across industries, this is emerging. In pharma: **Interesting signal. No pattern yet.**

**Challenge**
- Most obvious explanation: a security vendor survey built to show a problem it sells against.
- What else could explain it: most "out of scope" actions may be small and caught by people.
- What would make us wrong: an independent survey finds most companies enforce agent limits. Or pharma commercial agents stay in drafting, with people approving every output.

**Next evidence before Friday**
- A second survey, not from a security vendor, repeats the confidence versus enforcement gap.
- A pharma commercial platform or services firm seeks an outside test for an agent that writes into the customer record or drafts content.
- A drug maker or agency posts a role to govern or audit AI agents in commercial work.

**State: WAIT FOR FRIDAY.** Cross-industry and vendor-sourced, with nothing in pharma yet.
