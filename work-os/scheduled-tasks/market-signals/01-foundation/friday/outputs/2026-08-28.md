**Unit of analysis:** McKesson (drug distribution and health services). Not a drug maker.

**Evidence**

It "has not determined that the incident is material" ([Form 8-K, a current-report filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission, August 28](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/927653/000092765326000247/mck-20260825.htm)). Observed.
- Its notice to customers, as reported: "the incident involved third-party applications and the unauthorized access and exfiltration of data" ([BleepingComputer, August 28](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/mckesson-discloses-breach-after-shinyhunters-claims-patient-data-theft/)). Observed.

**Evidence strength: MEDIUM. REINFORCING INDICATOR.** A public company confirmed the theft in writing. Platforms and counts rest on the criminals' word.

**Obvious explanation:** one criminal group working through rented software, one phone call at a time.

**Hypothesis update**

Primary: Foundation (access, sign-on, vendor dependence). Secondary: Operating Model (who owns help desk resets and app approvals).

**Pattern basis:** fifth week running for health companies: Amgen, a billing vendor, Baxter, now McKesson. Same group as Baxter, which still "has not confirmed the nature of the stolen data" ([HIPAA Journal, named for the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, August 26](https://www.hipaajournal.com/shinyhunters-baxter-international-data-breach/)), but a separate break-in, so counted as new. ## Non-obvious analysis

**KEY INSIGHT: The sellers' counts cannot be added up, and that is the problem**
- "Committed," "live," "customers," and "using" are four different things. A leader reading both releases cannot tell who runs what.
- If big companies hold both, a doctor can have two records with two owners. That is where "our data does not match" starts. Inferred.

**KEY INSIGHT: One login, two doors**
- At McKesson, the group claims one sign-on opened both the customer relationship system and the data platform. Reviews cover each system. The shared login is a connection nobody reviews as a system.
- "The pieces have owners. The connections often do not." Here the connection is the sign-on and the help desk that resets it. Inferred.

**KEY INSIGHT: Plugging agents in got cheap. Checking what is behind the plug did not.**
- In one week, Veeva put an agent in front of a full field team, and Salesforce put its customer system inside Claude for "select pilot customers" ([Salesforce, August 26](https://www.salesforce.com/news/press-releases/2026/08/26/salesforce-and-anthropic-announce-claudeforce/)). A trade piece the day before: Model Context Protocol, the standard way agents connect to data, "says nothing about whether governed metrics exist behind it" ([CTO Magazine, August 25](https://ctomagazine.com/semantic-layer-ai-agents-iceberg-mcp/)).
- Each new agent on the same login also widens what one stolen login reaches. Signals 1 and 2 meet here. Inferred.

## Challenge the interpretation

- **What else could explain this?** Signal 1: a crime wave across industries, driven by phone scams, closer to Operating Model. Signal 2: Salesforce's "customers" may use it for patient services, not field sales. Then there is no split.
- **Counterevidence:** McKesson has not named Salesforce or Snowflake, and has not found the incident material. One agent across a full field team is real adoption.
- **What would make us wrong:** McKesson says the data was not from these platforms. A big drug company names one system and a clean switch.
